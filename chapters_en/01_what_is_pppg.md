# What Is PPPD: Symptoms, Causes, and Why You're Here

*Spoiler alert: nothing catastrophic is happening to you. Your hardware is intact. It's your software that's glitching.*

---

## The Short Answer

PPPD is what happens when you feel like you're rocking, swaying, spinning inside, dealing with visual swimming, jelly legs, a heavy head, and an unstable floor—yet every single medical test comes back normal. Clean MRI. Normal bloodwork. Healthy inner ears. Working eyes.

Doctors shrug their shoulders. That doesn't make it any easier on you—because the swaying feels completely, brutally real.

PPPD stands for **Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness**. In the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), it carries the official code **AB32.0**. This isn't something psychologists made up, and it isn't "all in your head." It is a recognized medical diagnosis.

Here is what matters most right now: PPPD is a **functional** disorder, not an organic or structural disease. Nothing inside your brain or inner ear is physically broken. What changed is **how those systems operate together**. The software glitched, while the hardware remains completely intact.

## PPPD Symptoms: The Complete Checklist

People with PPPD describe their sensations in dozens of different ways. If you recognize yourself in at least three or four items below, you're in the right place.

**Core Symptoms:**
- Unsteadiness and wobbliness when walking
- "Cotton-wool" or "jelly" legs
- Feeling like the floor is rocking, pitching, or dropping out from under your feet
- Brain fog, a "head stuffed with cotton" sensation, or heavy-headedness
- Internal swaying or rocking, even while sitting still or lying down
- Visual "swimming" or drifting when you move your eyes
- Symptom flare-ups in supermarkets, shopping malls, and subway stations
- Visual discomfort from busy floor patterns, stripes, or scrolling on your phone

**Associated Symptoms:**
- Anxiety and panic attacks
- Derealization—viewing the world "through thick glass" or "like a dream"
- Depersonalization—feeling detached from your own body
- Insomnia and light, unrefreshing sleep
- Morning unsteadiness (often worst right after waking up)
- Chronic tightness and pain in the neck, shoulders, and jaw
- Ringing in the ears (tinnitus) when anxiety spikes
- Light sensitivity and intolerance to bright or fluorescent lighting
- Compulsive "body scanning"—constantly monitoring yourself for symptoms

**What Is NOT a Symptom of PPPD:**
- Fainting or loss of consciousness
- Sudden, violent room-spinning vertigo lasting 5–60 seconds (that points to **BPPV**)
- Hearing loss in one ear
- Double vision
- Slurred speech or difficulty swallowing

If you experience anything on that last list, see a physician first. PPPD is a diagnosis of exclusion.

## A Quick History (Zero Fluff)

For decades, patients like us barely existed on medicine's radar. You'd walk into a clinic, tell the doctor, "I feel like I'm swaying," and get brushed off with, "It's just nerves." End of story. Invisible.

In the 1980s, clinicians called it *Phobic Postural Vertigo*. Later came *Space and Motion Discomfort* (which sounds like a diagnosis for astronauts) and *Chronic Subjective Dizziness*. Finally, in **2014**, international neuro-otology experts unified all those scattered pieces into a single clear definition. In **2017**, the Bárány Society published five precise diagnostic criteria.

Since then, PPPD has been recognized worldwide as a legitimate functional vestibular disorder. Not "bad nerves." Not "imagining things." A concrete clinical diagnosis with its own medical code.

## What Causes PPPD: How the Glitch Happens (In Tech Terms)

Let me explain this the way I explain it to everyone.

Your physical body is **hardware**—brain tissue, inner ears, eyes, muscles, nerves. Your mind, threat-detection circuits, and neural pathways are **software**.

PPPD isn't broken parts. It's a **software glitch**.

Picture your computer with its antivirus program (anxiety) pinned at 100% CPU usage. It fanatically scans every single file (every normal sensation in your body) hunting for a lethal threat. The CPU overheats. The cooling fan howls. The whole system starts lagging and freezing.

Instead of dialing back the runaway antivirus, you sprint to the repair shop to replace the hard drive. Then you swap out the RAM. Then the motherboard. Nothing fixes it—because nothing was ever wrong with the hardware.

**The problem is software that crashed under chronic overload. Our job is to reinstall and recalibrate it.**

## Your Three Balance Sensors

Your brain relies on three sensory systems to maintain balance:

1. **Eyes** (vision) — tell you where up and down are and whether the world around you is moving.
2. **Vestibular system** (the gyroscope in your inner ear) — detects head turns, tilts, and acceleration.
3. **Proprioception** (body position sense) — sensors in your muscles, joints, and feet tell your brain where your limbs and head are in space.

Normally, your brain cross-checks data from all three channels automatically on autopilot. You never think about it—any more than you consciously manage your breathing or heartbeat.

When you stay trapped in prolonged distress—unresolvable life pressure, exhaustion, chronic stress—your brain flips into **LETHAL DANGER** mode. Cortisol and adrenaline flood your bloodstream. Your muscles lock into spasm. And that's where the real trouble starts.

## The Pinched-Wire Neck Metaphor

This is a pivotal mechanism. Remember it.

Sensory receptors deep in your neck muscles—proprioceptors—report the exact position of your head to your brain. When chronic stress clamps your neck into a vise grip, those receptors get **squeezed right along with the muscle**—like a wire pinched in a doorframe.

Their signal reaches the brain with a tiny micro-delay.

Your eyes see: *We're walking forward.*
Your inner-ear gyroscope senses: *We're moving.*
Your locked-up neck reports a split second late: *Wait—we're still standing in place!*

Your brain interprets that **millisecond** mismatch as dizziness and unsteadiness. You feel "cotton wool" in your head. A sudden drop sensation. The illusion that the floor is shifting beneath your feet.

**Panicked, you rush off for an MRI—and the MRI comes back completely clean. Because the problem isn't structural damage. The problem is a desynchronized signal.**

## Why Massage Only Helps for a Day or Two

Plenty of people book a massage therapist. The therapist works out their rock-hard neck. Things feel noticeably steadier for a day or two—and then every symptom comes right back.

Why? Because you're rubbing a spasmed muscle locally **while the command to tense it keeps firing nonstop from a frightened brain**. That's like mopping up a puddle on the floor without turning off the leaking faucet. Pointless on its own.

Don't panic. It's a **circuit breaker**.

## PPPD Subtypes: Which One Do You Have?

PPPD isn't a single uniform experience—it shows up differently from person to person. Clinicians generally see three main patterns:

**1. Posturally Predominant:**
Unsteadiness ramps up whenever you're upright—standing or walking—and eases significantly when sitting or lying down. This is a classic presentation: your brain stops trusting proprioception and locks your leg and back muscles rigid, trying to balance on manual control.

**2. Visually Predominant:**
Your primary trigger is complex visual motion: supermarkets, patterned tile floors, scrolling on your phone, or scenery flashing past a bus window. Your visual system is overloaded while your inner-ear vestibular input is down-weighted—leaving your brain unable to filter out visual noise.

**3. Mixed:**
A combination of both patterns: standing upright feels unsteady *and* busy visual environments throw you off balance. Roughly 60% of people with PPPD have this mixed presentation.

Pinpoint your pattern now so you can prioritize your daily practice. If visual environments trigger you most, focus heavily on Chapter 8 (**Visual Dependence**) and VOR gaze-stabilization exercises. If upright posture hits hardest, prioritize Chapter 6 (**Vestibular Calibration**) and proprioceptive retraining.

## Is It Definitely PPPD? Differential Diagnosis

Before working through this manual, verify that what you're dealing with is PPPD rather than an active inner-ear or neurological condition. Compare the clinical patterns below:

| Feature | PPPD | BPPV | Ménière's Disease | Vestibular Migraine |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Type of dizziness** | Unsteadiness, rocking, brain fog | Brief room-spinning vertigo | Spinning attacks + ear roaring/fullness | Vertigo or rocking + headache/migraine signs |
| **Duration** | Hours to all day (persistent) | Seconds (triggered by head turns) | Minutes to hours (discrete attacks) | Minutes to hours/days (discrete attacks) |
| **Link to posture** | Worse standing and walking | Triggered rolling over or tilting head in bed | Independent of posture | Independent of posture |
| **Visual triggers** | Yes (busy stores, patterns) | No | No | Sometimes |
| **Worsened by anxiety** | Yes, consistently | No | No | Sometimes |
| **Hearing loss** | No | No | Yes | No |
| **MRI / routine labs** | Normal | Normal | Characteristic audiometric/inner-ear changes | Often normal |
| **Primary treatment** | CBT + vestibular rehabilitation (VRT) | Epley maneuver | Dietary management + medication | Migraine prevention & treatment |

> **Important:** If you have hearing loss, ringing or roaring in one ear, sudden attacks of true room-spinning vertigo, or fainting spells, **that is not PPPD**—see a neuro-otologist promptly. PPPD is a diagnosis of exclusion made once structural causes have been ruled out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**"My scan showed a disc bulge / 'cervical osteochondrosis' / 'VSD.' Could my dizziness be coming from that?"**
No. Routine age-related neck wear ("cervical osteochondrosis") appears on imaging in 90% of adults over 30, and disc bulges show up in 70% of people with zero symptoms. In the vast majority of cases, they are incidental radiology findings with no clinical link to dizziness. And "VSD" (a post-Soviet catch-all label for autonomic dysfunction) doesn't exist in either ICD-10 or ICD-11—it's simply an outdated wastebasket label for anxiety-driven autonomic dysregulation.

**"What if I have impaired blood flow to my brain?"**
If you're under 60 and don't have uncontrolled high blood pressure, diabetes, or advanced atherosclerosis, the probability of chronic vascular dizziness is virtually zero. A neck-vessel duplex ultrasound will come back normal—or report an "S-shaped curve / tortuosity of the vertebral artery," a harmless congenital variant found in roughly 40% of healthy adults.

**"My doctor prescribed a stack of nootropics. Will they cure this?"**
Mexidol, Actovegin, Cerebrolysin, vinpocetine (Cavinton), betahistine—not one of these medications has proven efficacy for PPPD. That doesn't mean you should stop prescribed drugs without speaking to your doctor, of course—just don't expect them to resolve PPPD. The only medications with solid clinical evidence in PPPD are **SSRIs/SNRIs** (covered in Chapter 13), and even those serve as a support tool alongside behavioral rehabilitation, never a replacement for it.
