# Vestibular exercises for PPPD: exercises for retraining the brain

*Why you stagger out of the blue and how to teach your brain to trust your inner gyroscope again, and not just your eyes.*

---

## 1. Essence and problems: life in the “pitching” mode

The most debilitating symptom of PPPD is the feeling that the ground is falling away from under your feet. You are standing on a hard floor, but it seems that you are slightly rocking from side to side, as if on the deck of a ship. In supermarkets with long colorful rows or in a crowd, this feeling turns into hell: the picture floats, your head becomes “cast iron”, and you want to quickly grab the cart or the wall. 

You look at your feet as you walk. You control your every step, constantly checking your balance. You are afraid to turn your head again or shift your gaze sharply. The nervous system is in constant visual supercontrol mode - you are trying to “maintain” balance with the power of your gaze.

## 2. Physiological explanation: gyroscope failure

Normally, our balance is maintained by three independent systems:
1. **Vestibular apparatus** (inner ear) - acceleration and head tilt sensor.
2. **Vision** - tells where the horizon line and surrounding objects are.
3. **Proprioception** (muscle sense) - pressure sensors in the feet and tension in the joints, reporting contact with the support.

The brain collects signals from these three channels and glues them together into a single picture of a stable world. 
When you get really stressed (or really dizzy), the calibration fails. The brain got scared and “turned off” trust in the vestibular apparatus and proprioception, considering their sensors unreliable. Instead, he turned up the vision sensitivity to 100%. This is called **visual addiction**. Now, when you walk, the brain tries to stabilize the body only due to the visual image. As soon as you go into a colorful supermarket, where the picture is constantly moving, or just close your eyes, your vision stops helping, and you start to sway wildly. 

The vestibular apparatus is absolutely physically healthy. He is simply “sleeping” because his brain has stopped listening to his signals. Our task is to forcefully wake him up and force the brain to trust the inner ear and feet again.

## 3. Cognitive traps: avoidance strategy and gaze fixation

The main cognitive trap here is the idea that if your head is spinning, you need to freeze and look at one point in order to stabilize the world. 
When you fix your gaze and avoid turning your head, you confirm the brain’s hypothesis: * “Yes, moving is dangerous. If we turn our heads, we will fall.”* The brain reinforces this mistake. To retrain the system, it must be purposefully introduced into a state of slight mismatch. **The brain only learns when it makes mistakes.** If you don't give it a reason to make mistakes and adjust its balance, it will never re-calibrate.

## 4. Point of Support: Insight

> Your vacillation is not a symptom of illness. This is a desperate attempt by the brain to maintain balance with closed eyes and pinched ears. We will open his eyes to reality.

---

## 5. Practice: Vestibular calibration

Each exercise should be done according to the principle: **“A little uncomfortable”**. If you start to feel nauseous or feel very stormy, reduce the pace or amplitude. We need a gentle challenge, not a panic attack.

Perform the complex 2 times a day (morning and evening). To be safe, stand next to a wall or a stable chair.

### Level 1: Statics (Turn on proprioception)
1. **Romberg stance:** Place your feet close to each other (toe to toe, heel to heel), lower your arms along your body. Take a deep breath. First, stand like this for 30 seconds with your eyes open. Then close your eyes for 30 seconds. Notice how the body begins to make small vibrations - it’s the feet and legs that are “groping” for balance. Don't bother them.
2. **Single Leg Stand:** Standing in the corner of the room (for safety), lift one leg and hold your balance for 30 seconds with your eyes open. Repeat for the other leg. When it becomes easy, try doing this with your eyes closed for at least 10 seconds.

### Level 2: Dynamics of the eyes and head (Separating vision and vestibular tissue)
1. **Look at a Point (Horizontal):** Extend your arm in front of you, raise your thumb to eye level (or choose a bright point on the wall). Without taking your eyes off the finger/point, begin to smoothly turn your head left and right (as if you were saying “no-no”). The finger should remain absolutely focused, and the background may be slightly blurred. Do it for 1 minute. Select the speed so that the picture does not double.
2. **Gaze at the Point (Vertical):** Same thing, but nod your head up and down (as if you are saying “yes, yes”) while keeping focus on your finger. Do it for 1 minute.

### Level 3: Gait Retraining
1. **Tandem Step:** Walk along an imaginary line (or along a tile/carpet joint), placing the heel of one foot close to the toe of the other. Keep your head straight, looking forward in front of you, not down at your feet. Walk 10–15 steps forward and backward like this.
2. **Walking with head turns:** Walk at a normal pace along the corridor. At every second step, smoothly turn your head to the left, then after two steps - to the right. This trains the brain to ignore dynamic noise from turning the head while walking.

---

### Homework (DZ):
1. Perform the complex **twice a day for 5 minutes**.
2. Get a progress tracker. Your goal for the first 2 weeks is to confidently hold the Romberg stance with your eyes closed for 30 seconds without panicking.
3. Stop looking at your feet when walking on the street. Lift your chin, look at the horizon, let your feet do the work.

---

## Level 4: Advanced exercises (join in 2-3 weeks)

When levels 1–3 become comfortable, add:

1. **Walking on uneven surfaces:** Walk onto a dirt path in a park, gravel path or lawn. Walk slowly, look at the horizon (not at your feet). The feet must adapt to uneven surfaces without visual guidance. Start with 5 minutes, increase to 15.
2. **Cushion Stand:** Stand with both feet on a sofa cushion or soft rug. Hold your balance for 30 seconds with your eyes open, then 15 seconds with your eyes closed. An unstable surface forces the brain to engage proprioception and the vestibular system.
3. **Walking with head turns + counting:** Walk at a normal pace and turn your head left and right every second step. At the same time, count backwards from 100 (100, 99, 98...). This loads cognitive resource and prevents the brain from getting stuck on controlling balance—putting it on autopilot.

## Progression table by week

| Week | Exercises | Goal |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **1–2** | Level 1 (static): Romberg + single leg stand | 30 seconds with eyes closed without losing balance |
| **3–4** | Level 2 (eyes/head): VOR horizontal + vertical | 1 minute without blurring the picture |
| **5–6** | Level 3 (gait): tandem step + walking with turns | 15 steps without support |
| **7–8** | Level 4 (Advanced): Rough Surface + Cushion | 30 seconds on a pillow with eyes closed |

## Integration into everyday life

Vestibular calibration is not just a 5-minute exercise in the morning and evening. You can train your brain **background** throughout the day:

* **In line at the store:** instead of standing tense and controlling your every step, shift your weight to one leg, wait for 20 seconds, change. Invisible stand on one leg.
* **In the kitchen:** when washing dishes, close your eyes for 15 seconds. Feel how your feet maintain balance. This is a micro-Romberg.
* **When walking:** consciously stop looking at your feet. Every time you catch yourself “looking at the asphalt,” lift your chin and look at the farthest building. In a week it will become a habit.
