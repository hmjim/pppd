# Lump in the Throat from Nerves: Why It Presses, Won't Go Down, and What Anxiety Has to Do with It

*You swallow — and something gets in the way. Like a piece of food is stuck. Or a ball. Or someone is gripping your throat. You go to an ENT — all clear. Gastroscopy — normal. Reflux ruled out. But the lump won't go away. And the more you think about it — the harder it presses.*

---

## "There's Definitely Something in My Throat"

You're not the first person to walk into an ENT's office and say: "Doctor, something is stuck in my throat. I feel pressure. It's hard to swallow. I'm afraid of choking."

The doctor examines your throat — all clear. Sends you for a gastroscopy — all clear. Checks your thyroid — normal. And then delivers the phrase that makes you want to clench your teeth: "It's just nerves."

And you get angry. Because the lump is **real**. You can feel it. It interferes with eating, swallowing, sometimes even breathing. What the hell do you mean, "just nerves"?

Okay, let's actually figure this out. No "it's just nerves" and no "have some chamomile tea."

---

## What's Happening in Your Throat: The Mechanics of the Spasm

What you're experiencing is called **globus sensation** (globus pharyngeus) in medicine. It's the feeling of a lump, pressure, or foreign body in the throat when there's no actual obstruction.

Here's what's happening at the muscle and nerve level:

**Your larynx is surrounded by muscles.** The cricopharyngeal muscle, pharyngeal muscles, hyoid bone muscles — a whole ensemble that works when you swallow, speak, and breathe. Normally, you don't even notice them.

When you've been living under stress for months, your body switches to "fight or flight" mode. Muscles throughout your body go on high alert. Your neck turns to stone. Your jaw clenches. And your laryngeal muscles — **go into spasm**.

But there's a nuance. The throat isn't just muscles. It's also **territory of suppressed emotions**.

Think about it: when you wanted to scream at your boss in fury — you stayed silent. When you wanted to burst into tears — you held it together. When you wanted to say "go to hell!" — you smiled and nodded.

Every swallowed scream, every unspoken phrase — is a muscular impulse that went to the throat and was blocked. The laryngeal muscles received the command "scream!" — and immediately received the counter-command "shut up!" Two opposing signals = spasm. Chronic spasm = the sensation of a lump.

Reich called this a **"throat block"** — part of the muscular armor. The unspoken word literally gets stuck in the throat.

---

## Why the Lump Gets Worse When You Think About It

The same mechanism kicks in here as with PPPD — the **adrenaline loop**:

1. You notice the lump → get scared → adrenaline
2. Adrenaline → muscles spasm harder → lump feels denser
3. Lump feels denser → "Maybe it's a tumor?!" → more adrenaline
4. More adrenaline → more spasm → "I'm going to choke!"

Sound familiar? It's the same loop as with dizziness. Just a different warning light. The brain chose the throat instead of the vestibular system. But the engine is the same: an overloaded nervous system.

## Lump in the Throat and PPPD: One Mechanism

If besides the lump you also have unsteadiness, brain fog, jelly legs, derealization — you don't have five diseases at once. You have **one** overheated nervous system sending signals through every available channel.

A lump in the throat is one of the most common companions of PPPD. Why? Because people with PPPD are typically chronic "nice guys": they endure, stay silent, swallow their resentment, avoid conflict. And the body pays for it with muscle spasm — in the neck, the throat, the jaw.

## What to Do About the Lump in Your Throat

### 1. Rule Out Organic Causes (Once)
ENT examination + gastroscopy (to rule out reflux) + thyroid ultrasound. Everything clear? Close the door to the clinic. Don't go back "just in case."

### 2. Stop Test-Swallowing
You do it constantly: you swallow to check — is the lump there or not. Every one of these "test swallows" focuses the brain on the throat and intensifies the spasm. It's like picking at a wound — it won't heal as long as you keep touching it.

### 3. Work on the Neck and Throat Muscles
[Jacobson's Progressive Relaxation](05_relaxation.html) — targeted tension/release of neck, shoulder, and jaw muscles. Yawning. Sighing. Gentle massage of the hyoid area. The goal — send the brain a reverse signal: "The throat is relaxed → there's no danger."

### 4. Say Out Loud What's Been Stuck
This isn't a metaphor. Literally: if you have an unspoken conflict, suppressed anger, words you've been swallowing for years — find a safe space and **say them out loud**. Write a letter you'll never send. Talk to a pillow. Scream in your car. The throat block often releases when the suppressed emotion finally finds an outlet.

### 5. Apply the "5-Minute Rule"
When the lump intensifies and panic hits — tell yourself: "This is an adrenaline loop. The throat muscles spasmed from fear. Adrenaline burns off in 5 minutes. I'm not going to choke. The airway is clear — the ENT confirmed it." More about the mechanics of the loop — [The Adrenaline Loop](09_adrenaline_loop.html).

---

## When Will the Lump Go Away

The lump doesn't disappear instantly — because the muscular armor took months or years to form. But with daily work (relaxation, exposure, processing suppressed emotions) — the lump starts to weaken within 3–6 weeks. First you stop noticing it for hours. Then for days. Then it's gone.

The main thing — stop fighting it. Stop checking. Stop being afraid. The lump feeds on your attention. Take away the attention — and it'll starve to death.
