# Inner child: who is really scared in your body 

*Why your unsteadiness is the tears of the frightened child inside you, how to stop being your own taskmaster and give yourself security.*

---

## 1. The essence and problems: a child locked in a basement 

If you suffer from PPPD and neurosis, you are probably used to communicating with yourself in the language of orders and strict demands. Your internal dialogue is an endless spanking: * “Get yourself together, you rag! Why are you unstuck again? You have to go, you have to work, you have to be strong!”*. 

You treat your body and your psyche like an evil slave owner treats a tortured horse: you beat it with a whip of demands, forcing it to run further, although it is already falling from fatigue. And when she starts stumbling (indicating dizziness and unsteadiness), you get angry at her for being “ineffective.” 

But ask yourself a question: who exactly is afraid inside you during a panic attack? Who cowers in fear when the store floor floats? This is not an adult, smart, 30- or 40-year-old person with a higher education and work experience. This is your **Inner Child**—the scared, vulnerable, emotional part of you. And while you stand over him with a whip and demand to “be a man / a strong woman,” his panic will only grow. 

## 2. Physiological explanation: the neurobiology of self-compassion and stress response 

Let's look at this from a neuroscience perspective. 

When you criticize yourself, demand the impossible, and scold yourself for your symptoms, your brain perceives your own inner voice as an **external threat**. 

The limbic system (amygdala) reacts to self-criticism in the same way as to the cry of a real enemy: it triggers a stress response, releases cortisol and adrenaline, tightening the neck muscles. Your inner taskmaster scares your amygdala 24/7. 

But our brain also has another system - the Caregiving system. 

When we experience genuine self-compassion, when we hug ourselves or speak calming words to ourselves, the hypothalamus releases **oxytocin** and **endorphins**. Oxytocin is a natural cortisol antagonist: it instantly reduces the activity of the amygdala, dilates blood vessels, relaxes muscles and returns the autonomic system to a state of rest. Your scared “inner child” (your limbic system) doesn’t need orders. He needs the security that only you can give him. 

## 3. Cognitive traps: “If I relax, I will become a doormat” 

The main cognitive trap of an excellent student: * “If I start feeling sorry for myself and indulging my “inner child,” I will completely fall apart, lie down on the sofa and turn into a vegetable. My toughness with myself is what keeps me going.”*

This is a fatal mistake. Rigidity led you to neurosis and PPPD. You are already at the bottom precisely because of her. Self-compassion is not self-pity, which is paralyzing. Self-compassion is recognizing the fact: * “I’m having a hard time right now. I'm scared. And that's okay. I will support myself."* A child who knows that his parent will protect him calmly goes out to explore the world. A child who is beaten at home for any mistake sits huddled under the bed. By giving yourself security, you will receive the resource to act, and not eternal paralyzing anxiety. 

## 4. Point of Support: Insight 

> Your unsteadiness is not a gyroscope failure. This is the scared child inside you who cries and tugs at your sleeve, trying to say: “I’m scared, I’m tired, stop!” And you respond by hitting him in the face and shouting: “Shut up and move on!” Stop being a tyrant to yourself. Become a Reliable Adult for him.

## 5. Practice: Calming the inner child 

Every time you are overwhelmed by unsteadiness or panic, instead of the usual anger (“Yes, how much you can spin!”*), use the support protocol: 

### Step 1: Role switching 
Mentally step out of the role of an “evil controller” and take the position of a “Reliable Adult.” Imagine that a small child (you are 5-7 years old) is standing in front of you, shaking with fear. 

### Step 2. Talk to the body (Focus) 
Address the frightened part within yourself with the following words (you can silently): 
> *“I see how scared you are. I'm here with you. You are not alone. I won't scold you for what makes you sick. It's just adrenaline, it's about to burn out. I will protect you. We are safe."* 

### Step 3. Physical anchor of oxytocin 
* Place a warm hand on your chest near your heart or hug your shoulders. 
* Take a slow, deep breath and a very long, relaxed exhale. Feel the warmth of your hand. This physical touch stimulates the release of oxytocin, calming the overheated amygdala at hormonal levels.

## “Safe Place” technique (visualization) 

This technique is used in trauma therapy to create an internal resource. It is especially useful when anxiety is running high and no logical arguments are working - because visualization addresses the limbic system directly, bypassing the rational brain. 

### How to do it: 
1. Lie down or sit comfortably. Close your eyes. Take 5 deep breaths. 
2. Imagine a place where you feel **absolutely safe**. This can be a real place (grandmother's dacha, the seashore from a vacation, your children's room) or a fictional one. 
3. Start “drawing” this place in detail: 
*What do you **see**? (colors, light, objects) 
*What do you **hear**? (silence, sound of waves, birdsong) 
* What do you **feel** on your skin? (warmth of the sun, coolness of the wind, softness of the blanket) 
* What is the **smell**? (freshly cut grass, sea air, grandma's pies) 
4. When the picture becomes bright and voluminous, invite your inner child there. Take his hand. Say: *“It is safe here. No fear can get here.”* 
5. Stay in this place for 5-10 minutes. 

**Why:** When you regularly “visit” a safe place in your imagination, your brain forms a neural connection between this image and a state of rest. After 2-3 weeks of practice, you will be able to “turn on” the safe place instantly - like a hot key - even while standing in line at the supermarket. 

## Self-Compassion Protocol by Kristin Neff 

Kristin Neff is a psychologist and creator of the scientifically based Mindful Self-Compassion program. Her method includes 3 components: 

### 1. Awareness (instead of merging) 
When you feel bad, don’t run away from the feeling and don’t drown in it. Just say: * “I'm in pain right now. I notice my pain.”* This simple confession is already therapy. It stops rumination and puts you in the position of an observer. 

### 2. Shared humanity (instead of isolation) 
Tell yourself: *“Suffering is part of the human experience. Millions of people right now feel the same way I do.”* This destroys the illusion “I’m the only one who is so broken”, which feeds neurosis. 

### 3. Self-kindness (instead of self-criticism) 
Place your hand on your chest (this activates the production of oxytocin) and say: * “I want to be kind to myself. I deserve care and sympathy."* If you can't tell it to yourself, tell it to your childhood photo. You definitely won’t refuse sympathy for this child. 

**Time:** 3-5 minutes. Do it every time you catch yourself thinking * “I’m a failure” *, * “Why can’t I just get up and live normally” * or * “Others are coping, but I’m not” *.
