# PPPD in Kids and Teens: When "Feeling Dizzy" Isn't Acting Out or Faking It

*Out of nowhere, your child starts bracing themselves against the hallway walls at school, complains of "brain fog," and begs you through tears to let them stay home. Doctors shrug, lab work comes back spotless, and their chart ends up with an insulting suspicion of faking it—or some throwaway "it's just anxiety" label. Take it from someone who's been through this hell firsthand: your kid isn't making it up. Their brain and balance system are simply stuck in alarm mode.*

## How PPPD Shows Up in Kids and Teens

In adults, **PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness)** feels like constant unsteadiness, walking on a spongy floor, or swaying on a boat deck. Kids don't have the vocabulary to describe subtle sensory glitches like that, so what you hear sounds very different. Instead of explaining a balance problem, they give you a blunt "I'm dizzy," "everything's spinning," or "I feel like I'm gonna throw up."

That's why it's so easy to mistake for laziness, defiance, or trying to get out of a test. In reality, the picture is made up of very specific signs:

- **School refusal.** Loud hallways between classes, people rushing past, harsh fluorescent lights, and having to sit upright at a desk overload the brain in seconds.
- **Sudden spike in motion sickness.** A kid who used to ride in the car or on the school bus without a problem starts turning pale and feeling short of breath five minutes into the drive.
- **Safety crutches (safety behaviors).** A sudden fear of being left alone in a room, going outside without an adult, or stepping into shopping malls.
- **Anxiety and autonomic nervous system glitches.** Morning stomachaches, heart-rate spikes, sweaty palms, and panic before every single trip out the door.

## Why Pediatricians and Neurologists Miss the Diagnosis

A standard clinic appointment lasts fifteen minutes, and chronic functional dizziness simply doesn't fit into that box. The doctor runs down the usual checklist: checks the ears, screens for anemia, orders an MRI, and performs positional testing for **BPPV** (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo).

When the scans come back clean, the inner-ear crystals are right where they belong, and the bloodwork is normal, the specialist often lands on a verdict: "They're just overly sensitive—making it all up." At best, your kid gets prescribed useless circulation pills; at worst, they get labeled a faker.

The truth is, with PPPD, the body's hardware is completely intact—only the software got scrambled. After a routine viral illness, a mild concussion, or a bad scare, an **adrenaline loop** kicks in: the brain starts checking every split second to see if the body is swaying. The harder a kid fears falling, the sharper the sense of unreality and unsteadiness becomes.

## Teen Triggers: Screens, Hormones, and School

A kid's nervous system is adaptable, but during puberty it's already running at redline. Often, it only takes a few overlapping factors to lock in a balance glitch and **visual dependence**:

- **Screens and rapid-fire feeds.** Hours spent glued to a phone or gaming overloads the visual cortex. The eyes get conditioned to a flat, fast-moving stream of pixels and lose their bearings in real, three-dimensional space.
- **Hormonal shifts.** Growth spurts, blood pressure fluctuations, and an unstable **autonomic nervous system** trigger brief waves of lightheadedness—which an anxious brain immediately logs as a threat.
- **Bullying and exam pressure.** Being targeted at school or terrified of failing exams keeps the body locked in survival mode. Chronic stress clamps the neck and shoulders into **muscle armor**, distorting signals from the muscles and amplifying that off-balance feeling.

## How Treating a Child Differs from the Adult Playbook

With adults, structured protocols work: breaking down the neurobiology, keeping **CBT** thought records, and doing monotonous gaze-stabilization drills in front of a blank wall. Force a teenager to do that, and they'll check out in three minutes flat. And with PPPD, boredom is the enemy—because the second their mind wanders, their attention snaps right back to scanning for symptoms.

That's why recovery for kids and teens takes a different approach:

- **Play-based Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT).** Instead of boring drills, bring in ping-pong, badminton, ball games, a balance board, juggling, or obstacle courses. The brain relearns how to hold its balance through play and engagement, not coercion.
- **Screen-time limits.** Without cutting back on phones and gaming consoles, the eyes will keep hijacking control from the inner ear. This isn't a punishment—it's a temporary off-ramp to let the nervous system decompress.
- **Addressing parental anxiety.** Anxiety is more contagious than any virus. If you're hovering in a panic, checking their blood pressure every half hour, and looking at them with terrified, pitying eyes, you're confirming their worst fear: "Something awful is happening to me."

## What You Can Do as a Parent Right Now

Your job isn't to throw random medications at your kid or try to brute-force them back to normal—it's to become their calm anchor.

1. **Don't dismiss what they're feeling.** Drop phrases like "stop making things up" or "just toughen up." Say it plainly: "I believe you that you feel dizzy. It's not dangerous damage—it's just a glitch in the settings, and we're going to help your brain get comfortable with movement again."
2. **Don't panic—and stop the diagnostic merry-go-round.** Endless repeat MRIs convince a teen that doctors just haven't found the hidden, fatal illness yet. Once a neurologist has ruled out acute pathology, close the chapter on hunting for another diagnosis.
3. **Give your kid back a sense of control.** Don't bubble-wrap them at home, but don't shove them straight into an eight-period school day through tears, either. Agree on a step-by-step **graded exposure** plan: first a walk around the block, then two classes, and a week later a full day. And expect a temporary **setback** right before a tough exam. That doesn't mean the condition is back—it's just a routine stress test of their new neural pathways.
