# Exit to life: your inner fulcrum 

*Final advice on why “slight lifting” when tired is your friend, not a disease, and how to transfer the locus of control inside yourself.*

---

## 1. Essence and problems: chick syndrome 

And now you have come to the end of this guide. You have done a tremendous job: you have restarted your body, learned to get out of adrenaline loops, turned off the internal scanner and sorted out your life areas. Your levels of unsteadiness and anxiety have noticeably decreased. 

But on the threshold of a new life, you may experience **chick syndrome**, which is afraid of falling out of the nest. 

You catch yourself thinking anxiously: * “What if the book ends and I’m left alone with my head? Where should I look for support? What will be my crutch now that I cannot rely on the author’s authority?”*. You are looking for an external guarantee, a magic seal that confirms that you are healthy forever. But there are no external guarantees. The only guarantee now lies within you. 

## 2. Physiological explanation: internal locus of control and overload sensor 

Recovery from PPPD comes when you shift your **locus of control** (source of responsibility) from outside to inside yourself. 

While you were running to doctors and looking for a “magic pill,” your locus of control was external. You felt like a helpless victim of circumstances. But now you have become your own neurologist, psychotherapist, and coach. You know the physiology of your symptoms thoroughly. You know how your brain works, why your neck spasms, how the VOR reflex works and how adrenaline burns out in 5 minutes. You are no longer a “patient.” You are an engineer of your nervous system who found a bug in the software and rewrote the code. 

Many people who have completely overcome PPPD and returned to a full life (travel, sports, work), sometimes remain with the so-called **"slight shaking"** or microsymptoms due to severe fatigue. 

This is not a sign of the disease returning. This is a **healthy physiological reaction** of the vegetative to overload. 

The average person gets a headache or a stomach ache when they don't get enough sleep. As someone who has gone through PPPD, your overload sensor is now a slight feeling of instability. This is your built-in indicator: *“Leather, we didn’t sleep for 2 days and drank a bucket of coffee. It's time to go to bed."* This sensor is your ally, protecting you from burnout again. There is no need to be afraid of him. 

## 3. Cognitive traps: the search for eternal stability 

Major Cognitive Trap: *“A healthy person never experiences dizziness, anxiety or fatigue. As long as I have even the slightest hint of symptoms, I am sick.”*

This is perfectionism transferred to health. Healthy people stagger in the wind, they get dizzy at altitude, they get tired, angry and anxious before important events. To be alive means to be dynamic, to experience changes in tone and emotions. Recovery is not about becoming a liquid metal terminator who doesn't feel pain. Recovery is the ability to live a full life *together* with your physiological reactions, without making drama out of them. 

## 4. Point of Support: Insight 

> I didn't give you a magic pill - it doesn't exist in nature. But I have given you something much more powerful - UNDERSTANDING. You are no longer a blind kitten rushing around clinics. Do you know the map of the area? You know where the holes are and how to get around them. Go and live your life. Fall, get up, make mistakes, but move forward. Life awaits you beyond this screen.

## Life after PPPD: what to expect 

### First month after stabilization 
You will notice that you think about dizziness less and less. A whole day may go by without you remembering him at all. And then in the evening you realize: * “Stop, I haven’t been thinking about unsteadiness all day!” * - and this in itself can cause mild anxiety. This is fine. The brain is accustomed to “sentinel mode” and it is unusual for it to turn off the scanner. 

**What to do:** Nothing. Just make a mental note: *“This is what recovery looks like. I felt good and forgot about it. This is the goal."* 

### Second month 
Forgotten desires will begin to return. You will want to go somewhere, meet friends, go to a restaurant, buy new clothes. This is a great sign - your limbic system has turned off survival mode and turned on life mode. The brain stopped saving resources for “saving from death” and began to direct them towards pleasure. 

**Possible pitfall:** You may start doing “too much too fast” - walking for 5 hours, working out every day, staying up late with friends. And after a week you get a kickback. It's not a disaster. This is calibration. The brain shows you a new acceptable load range. Just slow down for a couple of days and carry on. 

### Third month and beyond 
At this point, PPPV is no longer the central theme of your life. You will remember him the way they remember a bad flu - * “Yes, it was hard. But it passed.”* You will notice that you have become a different person: more aware, less anxious, better understanding your body and your boundaries. 

Many people who have gone through PPCP have a natural desire to **help others** - to share their experience, to support those who have just begun the journey. This is a healthy impulse. But be careful not to turn helping others into a new form of hypercontrol. You don't have to save the world. You have to live your life. 

### About residual symptoms 

Let's say this again because it's important. 

Many people, after complete recovery from PPPD, **are left with a slight “overload sensor”**. This may manifest itself as: 
* Slight feeling of instability due to severe lack of sleep. 
* Micro-shakyness under high stress (conflict at work, exam, flight). 
* Fleeting “swimming” during ARVI or when you are sick. 

This is **not a relapse of PPPD**. This is a normal vegetative reaction of your particular body to overload. Some people get a headache when they're stressed, others get a twist in their stomach, and you get a slight swaying. This is your individual “fuel level indicator”.

The correct reaction to it: 
1. **Don’t be scared.** *“Oh, hello, a familiar feeling. So I'm overloaded."* 
2. **Follow the protocol:** go to bed early, put away the coffee, do Jacobson. 
3. **Move on with your life.** In 1–2 days everything will pass.
