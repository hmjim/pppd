# Psychosomatic dizziness: when every test is normal but the floor keeps moving

*You've had an MRI, a Doppler ultrasound, blood work for everything under the sun, visited three neurologists and two ENTs. The verdict is unanimous: "You're healthy." Yet you're swaying like you're on the deck of a ship in a force-nine storm. Let's figure out what's actually broken and why it's fixable.*

---

## You're not crazy. And you're not faking it.

Let's get this straight immediately. If you're reading this page, you've probably already done the rounds. MRI of the brain — clean. Neck vessel ultrasound — normal. Blood work — perfect. The otoneurologist spun your head, watched your eyes — "Vestibular system is fine."

And yet you're swaying. Every single day. The floor shifts in the supermarket. Your legs turn to jelly on the escalator. In the subway the walls close in. You walk down the street feeling drunk without having had a single drop.

The most infuriating part? You go to the neurologist and he says: "Reflexes are normal. Gait is fine. MRI is clear. Try some vitamins." But you ARE swaying. Every. Single. Day.

And so you google "psychosomatic dizziness" or "psychogenic vertigo," because the doctors shrugged and you're grasping at the last straw: maybe it's from stress?

Yes. It is from stress. But not in the cliché sense of "all diseases come from nerves, drink some chamomile tea." This is a specific, well-studied, documented malfunction in the nervous system. And it has a name.

---

## What it's really called

What people call "psychosomatic dizziness" or "dizziness from nerves" has a precise name in modern medicine: **PPPD** (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness), ICD-11 code AB32.0.

This is a **functional disorder**. Not organic. The difference is fundamental:

- **Organic** — the hardware is broken. A tumor, stroke, displaced otoliths. Visible on scans.
- **Functional** — the hardware is intact, but the software crashed. Like a computer with perfectly good components whose operating system is stuck in an infinite reboot loop.

Your brain, vestibular apparatus, blood vessels and nerves are **physically intact**. But the software that processes balance signals switched to emergency mode. And this emergency mode isn't "in your head." It's in your neurophysiology.

---

## Why you're swaying: the mechanics in 3 minutes

To keep you standing upright, the brain simultaneously processes three data streams:

1. **Eyes** — "I can see the walls are vertical and the floor is horizontal."
2. **Vestibular apparatus** (inner ear) — "Head is tilted 2 degrees to the right."
3. **Proprioception** (sensors in muscles and joints) — "Right foot bears more weight, neck is turned."

In a healthy person, the brain merges these three signals into a unified picture automatically. You don't think about balance — it just works. Like autopilot in an airplane.

Now imagine: you've spent six months in chronic stress. Sleep-deprived. Anxious at work. Swallowing anger at your partner. Worrying about the future. Your nervous system has been running at full capacity for months.

At some point the brain **switched off autopilot**. It decided: "The situation is dangerous, switch to manual control." Now you're controlling your balance consciously. Walking "with the handbrake on." And manual control is always worse than automatic. You start monitoring every micro-sway of your body. And the brain, instead of filtering out normal walking oscillations, **interprets them as a threat of falling**.

Result: you sway. For real. Physically. But the cause isn't in the ear or the blood vessels. The cause is that the brain has forgotten how to walk on autopilot.

## Symptoms: recognize yourself

Here's what psychosomatic dizziness typically looks like. Not every item is mandatory — some people have three from the list, others all ten:

- Rocking, as if on a ship deck. Not spinning (that's BPPV), but **swaying, unsteadiness, a sense of the floor shifting**
- In supermarkets, shopping centers, or open spaces — immediately worse. Colorful shelves, bright lights, crowds — and you're overwhelmed
- Mornings after waking are the hardest. By evening it usually eases
- "Cotton wool" head, fog, hard to concentrate on text
- Legs feel alien. Heavy. Numb. "Walking through mud"
- Walls "breathe," the floor "sinks," the world feels unreal (this is called [derealization](40_derealization_pppg.html))
- Fear of falling, fainting, "switching off" in the middle of the street
- Gets worse with fatigue, sleep deprivation, on an empty stomach, after coffee
- In a calm home environment — manageable. Step outside — it hits

Sound familiar? Then I have two pieces of news for you.

The bad news: this won't go away on its own if you just wait and hope.

The good news: **this is fully reversible**. Because the hardware is intact. You just need to reinstall the software.

---

## Three reasons this happened to you

### 1. Muscle armor

When a person lives under stress for months, their body turns into a monolith. Neck like stone. Shoulders up to the ears. Jaw clenched. You don't notice it anymore — you're used to it.

But clamped neck muscles **distort the proprioceptive signals** going to the brain. The brain receives garbage data from neck sensors and can't reconcile it with data from the eyes and vestibular system. Result — desynchronization. You sway. More details in the chapter [Muscle Armor](04_muscle_armor.html).

### 2. The adrenaline loop

You swayed → you got scared → adrenaline hit the bloodstream → muscles clamped even harder → swaying tripled → you got even more scared → another dose of adrenaline. An infinite cycle. Like a script stuck in an endless `while(true)`. More in the chapter [The Adrenaline Loop](09_adrenaline_loop.html).

### 3. Hypercontrol

You stopped trusting your body. Now you're monitoring 24/7: "Am I swaying? Am I not? What about now? What if I turn my head?" This constant monitoring **by itself** keeps the nervous system on high alert. The brain thinks: "If the boss keeps checking balance — there must be a real fall threat. Maintain defenses!" And it defends with swaying.

## What actually works (and what doesn't)

### ❌ What DOESN'T work:
- **Nootropics** (piracetam, cerebrolysin) — useless for functional disorders. Nothing to "nourish," the hardware is fine
- **Vascular IV drips** — the blood vessels aren't the problem
- **Endless examinations** — each new "just in case" doctor visit feeds the anxiety
- **Betahistine** — works for Meniere's disease, not for PPPD

### ✅ What WORKS:
- **[Vestibular exercises](06_vestibular.html)** — retrain the brain to trust autopilot again. Daily, minimum 3 months
- **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)** — breaks the "symptom → fear → symptom amplification" loop. More: [CBT for dizziness](34_cbt_for_dizziness.html)
- **[Jacobson's Progressive Relaxation](05_relaxation.html)** — releases the muscle armor, restores neck mobility
- **Exposure therapy** — gradual return to frightening places (stores, transport, crowds) instead of avoidance
- **Physical activity** — running, swimming, strength training. Burns cortisol, reboots the autonomic system
- **SSRIs (antidepressants)** — prescribed by a psychiatrist for severe anxiety. Not a crutch, but scaffolding during renovation

---

## How long does recovery take

With daily work — exercises, exposure, mindset work — the average timeline for recovery from psychosomatic dizziness is **3–6 months**. Not three days. Not "take pills for a week and it's gone." Three to six months of daily, methodical work.

I walked this path myself. Six months of swaying, derealization, panic. And six months back. Every day — exercises, exposure, anxiety work. Now I'm 100% healthy. No residual symptoms. No "well, sometimes it sways a little." Complete zero.

It's possible. But not on pills and not on the couch.
