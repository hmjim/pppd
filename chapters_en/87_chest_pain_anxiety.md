# Chest Pain from Anxiety: When You're Sure You're Dying, but the ECG Is Normal

A stab in your chest. Your heart lurches. Your left arm goes slightly numb. You dialed 911 and sat on the floor, back pressed against the wall, saying your goodbyes to life.

The paramedics arrived. They ran an ECG. The doctor looked at it and said: "Everything's fine, try some valerian." You didn't believe him.

That was the fifth time. Or the tenth. Cardiologist, Holter monitor, echocardiogram, treadmill stress test — all clear. But your chest keeps hurting. And you keep thinking: "They just didn't find it."

## Where the Pain Comes From If Your Heart Is Healthy

Between your ribs are the intercostal muscles. When you're anxious, your body is tense. Shoulders hiked up to your ears. Jaw clenched. Breathing shallow. These muscles spend hours in spasm and start to ache.

The pain is sharp, stabbing, sometimes pressing. It radiates to the left arm, shoulder blade, neck. Sounds like a heart attack? Very much so. Is it a heart attack? No.

I used to lie in bed at night counting my pulse, fingers pressed to my neck. Every other beat felt "wrong." My chest felt like it was being squeezed. I was sure: this is the end. But it was just clenched muscles and panic.

There's also precordial catch syndrome — a sudden sharp pain on the left side, like being jabbed with a needle. It lasts from a second to a couple of minutes. Scares you half to death. The cause is a fleeting spasm or a pinched nerve in the chest wall. Completely harmless, but it feels like someone drove a knife in.

The third possibility — hyperventilation. During anxiety, you breathe fast and shallow. Carbon dioxide in the blood drops. Blood vessels constrict. You get a squeezing sensation in the chest, tingling in the hands, dizziness. The brain interprets all of this as a "heart attack" — and panic intensifies. The circle closes. Panic → hyperventilation → symptoms → even more panic.

## Why Your Brain Doesn't Trust the Doctors

Because the pain is real. You're not making it up. You genuinely feel the pressure, the stabbing, the burning. And when the doctor says "it's nerves" — your anxious brain translates that as "he didn't find it, I need another doctor."

The anxiety system doesn't accept the answer "everything's fine." It needs an explanation for why it hurts. "Muscle spasm from stress" doesn't sound serious enough. Not scary enough.

I saw a cardiologist five times in three months. Every time the ECG was perfect. Every time I walked out thinking: "Okay, dodged the bullet today, but next time they'll find something for sure."

That's not rational thinking. It's anxiety hijacking your logic. Like a virus that intercepts screen output and shows you whatever it wants.

## When You Should Actually Be Concerned

I'm not going to pretend that chest pain is always just nerves. Sometimes it is the heart. Here are the red flags that warrant calling an ambulance:

- Pressing, squeezing pain behind the breastbone that doesn't go away for more than 15–20 minutes
- Pain that started during physical exertion and didn't go away after stopping
- Along with the pain — cold clammy sweat, nausea, gray face
- Pain radiates to the jaw (specifically the jaw — this is a heart-specific symptom)
- You're over 45, you smoke, you have diabetes, or there's a family history of early heart attacks

If none of that applies, and the pain is stabbing, changes with position, gets worse when you press your finger on the spot, appears at rest, and goes away when you get distracted — it's not your heart. It's muscles and nerves irritated by anxiety.

Simple test: press your finger on the spot that hurts. If it hurts more — it's definitely not your heart. Cardiac pain comes from inside; it can't be made worse by pressing from outside.

Cardiac pain doesn't depend on position. It doesn't get worse when you press the sore spot. And you can't distract yourself from it with an interesting movie.

## The Myth: "If It Hurts, I Should Take It Easy"

Avoiding physical activity with cardiac neurosis is a trap that almost everyone falls into.

The more you're afraid — the less you move. The less you move — the weaker the muscles, the worse the circulation, the shallower the breathing. The body becomes deconditioned — and any effort triggers tachycardia.

The brain reads tachycardia as "the heart is broken." A new dose of fear. Even less movement. An even weaker body. Another circle.

Cardiologist gave you the green light? ECG is clean? Then walk, run, swim. The heart is a muscle. It needs work, not cotton wool.

## The Key Insight

Chest pain from anxiety is the most frightening of all nervous symptoms. Because culture has hammered a simple formula into our heads: "chest pain = heart = death." But if the cardiologist has checked and given you the all-clear — trust him. Don't Google. Don't seek a second opinion from a fifth cardiologist.

Your heart is healthy. It's not what's hurting — what's hurting is the nervous system that's stuck in panic mode and is spasming everything in sight.
