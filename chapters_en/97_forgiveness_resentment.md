# Chronic Resentment: Why Unsettled Scores Keep Your Autonomic Nervous System on a Leash

*Maybe you’ve already worked through your anger, you’re doing your exercises, and you no longer flinch at the sight of a grocery store—yet a thick, heavy residue still lingers inside. You keep replaying old conversations with doctors, dismissive remarks from loved ones, and your own past missteps, without noticing how that mental loop tilts your horizon all over again every single day. Resentment isn’t just a bad mood; it’s a quiet physiological brake that keeps your brain from believing the danger is over.*

## 1. The Problem

When emotions come up in **PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness)**, almost everyone confuses acute anger with chronic resentment. Anger is straightforward: it’s a fast flash, an impulse to defend your boundaries and discharge tension through action. Sure, suppressed anger gets locked into **muscle armor**, but on its own, anger burns fast and short.

Resentment works differently. **Resentment is an unfinished emotional transaction.** It’s an unpaid invoice you billed to the world, to someone else, or to yourself—and nobody is paying up. Instead of writing off a bad debt, you open your internal ledger every single day, check the balance, and fume because the register is still empty.

For someone with PPPD, that ledger almost always revolves around three targets:

- **Doctors.** Endless exam rooms, expensive MRIs, useless IV drips, and the standard brush-off: “Everything looks normal—it’s just anxiety.” You walk away feeling like they took your money and left you to drown. When I was climbing out of PPPD myself, I spent months running a mental courtroom against a couple of neurologists, shoving test results in their faces in my head and proving they got it wrong.
- **Loved ones.** The most painful category. PPPD doesn’t show up on a scan. Your partner gets worn down by your limitations, family members tell you to “just pull yourself together,” and friends can’t grasp why someone who looks completely healthy is terrified of a shopping mall. Inside, a bitter sense of betrayal builds up: “I’m in the deepest hole of my life, and you don’t even believe I’m sick.”
- **Yourself.** The quietest and most toxic form of resentment. “Why did I run myself into the ground?” “Why didn’t I stop sooner?” “How did I let my brain break like this?” Here, you’re your own judge, defendant, and jailer.

As long as even one of these tabs stays open, you’re walking into **Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT)** with a ball and chain around your ankle: you’re doing the drills, but part of your brain’s bandwidth stays bolted to the past.

## 2. The Neuroscience

To your nervous system, an unfinished transaction is **a stress response with no discharge**. When a threat or injustice hits, the sympathetic branch of your **autonomic nervous system** mobilizes the body: it dumps adrenaline and cortisol and locks down the deep muscles of your neck and back. Normally, that cycle closes with action—fighting, fleeing, or drawing a hard boundary—and then the parasympathetic branch brings the system back to baseline. With resentment, action is blocked: you couldn’t prove your point to the doctor, you bit your tongue with your partner, and you can’t outrun your past self. Your autonomic nervous system gets stuck in idle-rev—foot on the gas, clutch pressed to the floor, engine overheating in neutral.

Then the neurobiology of resentment kicks in. fMRI studies on social pain have shown a direct overlap: ruminating on resentment lights up the **anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** and the **anterior insula**—the exact same regions that fire during acute **physical pain**. To your brain, there’s no meaningful difference between “I just got hit” and “I was treated unfairly and I’m still chewing on it.” Your brain literally hurts.

For PPPD, that’s critical. The insula isn’t just an emotional pain center—it’s the brain’s main hub for interoception, sitting right next door to the vestibular cortex, helping evaluate balance and triggering autonomic misfires. When **CAS (Cognitive Attentional Syndrome)** feeds the brain resentment for hours on end, the insula and anterior cingulate cortex never cool down:

- An overloaded insula misreads normal, everyday body sway as a threat.
- **Visual dependence** ramps up: an anxious brain stops trusting its internal sensors and clings to your eyes for stability.
- The **adrenaline loop** spins up: stress from resentment amplifies dizziness → your brain panics at the symptom → anxiety pumps more adrenaline → you feel even wobblier.

That’s why lingering resentment makes it so easy to trigger a **setback**: you may be doing your **graded exposure**, yet one mental shouting match with the past snaps your autonomic nervous system right back into a fighting stance.

## 3. Cognitive Traps

Resentment stays lodged in your head thanks to a few classic cognitive distortions targeted by **CBT** and **MCT (Metacognitive Therapy)**:

1. **“As long as I’m resentful, I’m right.”** It feels like letting go means admitting the doctor was right or minimizing your own pain. But the universe doesn’t keep a complaint log, and your tension proves nothing to anyone. You’re the one drinking the poison, hoping the other person gets sick.
2. **The “just forgive” trap.** The worst piece of advice in pop psychology. Trying to plaster on a smile, “thank your illness,” and mentally hug the person who hurt you while you’re boiling inside doesn’t settle the score. **Forced forgiveness is just another form of suppression**, driving the stress response deeper into your autonomic nervous system and sparking another wave of symptoms.
3. **The illusion of controlling the past.** Part of CAS is replaying old conversations in hopes of finding the knockout comeback that will retroactively undo what happened. As a result, your working memory gets burned on simulating the past instead of adapting to the present.
4. **Resentment as safety crutches.** As long as you keep people at arm’s length through bitterness (“nobody can be trusted”), you don’t have to take risks again, trust again, step out of isolation, or own your next steps.

## 4. The Turning Point

The shift starts the moment you draw a hard line between **forgiveness** and **excusing**. Closing the tab on resentment doesn’t mean saying “they were right” or “it was no big deal.” That doctor really may have been incompetent, your loved one’s words really may have been cruel, and your past choices really may have been mistakes. The harm is real.

**Letting go of resentment isn’t a gift to the person who wronged you—it’s a unilateral termination of a toxic contract for the sake of your nervous system.**

If someone totals your car in a hit-and-run, you can stand on the shoulder for five years screaming after them—or you can log the loss, get another ride, and keep moving. Resentment is refusing to leave the shoulder. You close the account not because they earned it, but because keeping an internal debt collector on payroll is too expensive—it’s costing you your balance.

## 5. Practice

No candles, affirmations, or spiritual fluff. We’re using psychologist Everett Worthington’s **REACH protocol**—one of the most thoroughly validated tools in evidence-based psychology for resolving stuck resentment.

Before you start, pick **one specific episode** and one person. Pen and paper are non-negotiable—if you try to do this in your head, it will quickly slide right back into your usual rumination.

### Step 1. R — Recall (Remember without spiraling)
Write down the episode in dry, bare facts, like a security camera: dates, exact words, actions—without labels like “he’s a jerk” or “I’m pathetic.” Then notice how your body reacts right now: tight throat, rock-hard neck, racing pulse. Don’t run from the sensation; just name it: “Yes, I feel bitter, and I acknowledge the real extent of the damage.”

### Step 2. E — Empathize (See the mechanics without excusing them)
Look at the other party not as a cartoon villain, but as a limited or frightened human system:
- The doctor in a 12-minute slot was running off an outdated playbook and was afraid to admit they didn’t know.
- Your loved one was scared by seeing you so helpless and threw up denial as a shield.
- Your past self acted with the exact bandwidth and knowledge you had at the time.
This doesn’t erase accountability, but it strips away the illusion of a personal vendetta against you.

### Step 3. A — Altruistic gift (A deliberate act of release)
Think of a time when you messed up, snapped at someone, or failed to see someone else’s pain, and you were given the grace to move forward. Lock in that feeling of relief. Now extend that same move deliberately—as a gift to your own central nervous system, taking off the uniform of a lifelong prison guard.

### Step 4. C — Commit (Lock in the closed account)
Give your brain a clear marker that the transaction is complete. At the bottom of the page, write a sober statement: *“I acknowledge that this episode was painful and unfair. Starting today, however, I am closing this account and stopping my demand for compensation from the past, for the sake of my health.”* Date and sign it.

### Step 5. H — Hold on (Hold steady through a mental setback)
True to **MCT**, a couple of days later your memory will serve up that old conversation again. That’s not a failure—it’s just an old neural habit. The moment you catch rumination starting:
1. Name the process: *“My brain just pulled out the old receipt again.”*
2. Don’t argue or re-litigate the case—the verdict is already in.
3. Shift your attention outward: feel your feet on the floor, rest your eyes on a real object in the room, and return to what you were doing.

## Homework

1. **Audit your open tabs.** Write down three active resentments: one toward the medical system, one toward someone close to you, and one toward yourself. Pick the most emotionally charged one.
2. **Put REACH on paper.** Carve out 25 quiet minutes and walk your chosen episode through all five steps of the protocol on paper, right down to the signed closing statement.
3. **Seven-day bounce-back tracker.** For the next seven days, every time this resentment pops back up, log a tally in your phone, tell yourself “Account closed,” and shift your focus to the ground under your feet and a concrete action. At the end of the week, check how much the replay frequency and baseline tension have dropped.
