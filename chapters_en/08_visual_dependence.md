# Visual addiction: unhooking from eye anchors

*Why supermarkets, subways and colorful asphalt cause panic and how to apply the “Horizon Rule” to restore stability.*

---

## 1. The essence and problems: the nightmare of supermarkets and colorful floors

Every person with PPPD fears the same thing: large, open spaces with lots of detail. A trip to the supermarket turns into torture. As soon as you enter the hall with high shelves littered with colorful boxes, the world begins to “float”. The motley tiled floor seems uneven, your legs feel weak, and your head feels foggy. 

The same story happens on the subway, at busy intersections, or even at home when scrolling through the news feed on your phone. You notice that your body instinctively shrinks, and your gaze is firmly fixed on the asphalt right in front of the toes of your shoes. You are afraid to look up, because it seems that if you stop looking at your feet, you will immediately stumble and fall.

## 2. Physiological explanation: optical flow and video card overload

This phenomenon in medicine is called **visual dependence**. 

When the brain, due to high levels of anxiety, stopped trusting signals from the inner ear and feet, it transferred 100% of balance control to the eyes. Your gaze has become a crutch. As long as you are in a small, familiar room with white walls, everything is fine. But as soon as you step outside, your retina is bombarded with a huge array of moving information. In science this is called **Optic Flow**. 

Cars are driving, people are walking, supermarket shelves are full of goods, tiles are flashing under your feet. In a healthy person, the brain filters out this “noise”, understanding: *they* are moving, not *I*, because the vestibular apparatus is working stably. But your vestibular signals are blocked by your brain. The brain focuses only on vision. Seeing the chaotic movement around, he falls into a stupor. It seems to him: “Everything around is moving, which means I’m moving too!” We are falling! An instant vegetative crisis occurs - a surge of adrenaline, nausea, unsteadiness and panic. Your internal computer simply freezes due to the overload of the “video card”.

## 3. Cognitive traps: a saving glance at the asphalt

The logic of the frightened brain is simple: * “Since the world is spinning around me, I will look strictly at my feet on the ground in order to control every step. It’s safer this way.”*

This is a major cognitive trap. Looking at your feet, you shorten your visual horizon to one meter. Your brain is deprived of a three-dimensional, three-dimensional perspective and ceases to see stable landmarks in the distance (buildings, trees, the horizon line). Without these reference points, your brain has an even harder time calculating balance, and it tightens your neck muscles even more. It turns out to be a vicious circle: you look at your feet so as not to fall ➔ your brain loses its bearings ➔ your unsteadiness intensifies ➔ you shrink and look at your feet even more closely.

## 4. Point of Support: Insight

> Your eyes are sensors for collecting information about the world, not anchor bolts with which you must fasten yourself to the asphalt. Let your legs move on their own; they have been able to do this since they were one and a half years old. Raise your head.

## 5. Practice: Breaking visual dependence

Our goal is to retrain the brain to process optical flow without panic and teach it to let go of visual control.

### 1. Horizon Rule
Every time you go outside:
* **Be aware of the clamp:** Notice how your head is tilted down, and your gaze scans the asphalt in search of holes and cracks.
* **Do a reset:** Relax your shoulders, open your teeth, gently lift your chin so that it is parallel to the ground.
* **Find the distant anchor:** Move your gaze 10–15 meters ahead or to the horizon line (roofs of houses, treetops). Walk, keeping your attention on this long-term perspective. Let your peripheral vision blur what's happening under your feet. Your feet will adapt to the unevenness of the road on their own thanks to proprioception if you stop disturbing them.

### 2. Display in a supermarket
There is no need to avoid stores. We will use them as a simulator:
* Go to the supermarket. As soon as you feel a little dizzy from the diversity of the rows, stop. Don't run towards the exit.
* Look for a static sign at the far end of the hall (for example, “EXIT”, “MILK” or a clock).
* Fix your gaze on this inscription. Exhale slowly. Stand and look at it for 1 minute, ignoring the flashing of people and goods on the sides. The picture around the periphery will begin to stabilize. When the hesitation subsides, keep moving, keeping your focus on global reference points rather than on individual boxes on the shelves.

## Protocol for working behind the screen

If you work on a computer, you are at risk. The screen creates an intense optical flow in a confined space: scrolling text, switching tabs, animations. For the visually dependent brain, this is a continuous vestibular trigger.

### Rules for working behind the monitor:
1. **20-20-20 Rule:** Every 20 minutes, look at an object 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20 seconds. This switches vision from near to distant focus and relieves spasm of the ciliary muscle.
2. **Font Size:** Increase the font in your editor/browser by 20-30%. Small text causes the brain to “clean” its eyes to the screen, increasing visual dependence.
3. **Smooth scrolling:** Turn off the scrolling animation in the settings (or scroll page by page rather than smoothly). Flashing lines is a powerful optical trigger.
4. **Dark theme:** Switch your IDE, browser and instant messengers to dark mode. Less brightness means less load on the visual cortex.
5. **Monitor position:** The top edge of the screen is at eye level or slightly below. Tilt of the head downward pinches the neck and increases unsteadiness.

### Exercise: Optokinetic Desensitization
This is an advanced technique for those who have a strong visual addiction (hard in stores, the subway, when scrolling the screen):

1. Open the “optokinetic stimulation” video on YouTube (black and white stripes moving horizontally or vertically).
2. Sit 1 meter away from the screen. Look at the center of the video, allowing the stripes to move around the periphery. Don't track them with your eyes - look “through” the screen.
3. Start with 30 seconds. If you don’t feel nauseous, increase to 1 minute, then to 2 minutes.
4. Do it once a day for 2 weeks.

**Why it works:** You train your brain to ignore a moving visual background by relying on vestibular and proprioceptive signals. This is essentially exposure for the visual cortex.
