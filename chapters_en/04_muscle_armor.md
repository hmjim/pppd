# Muscle armor in PPPD: how chronic tension causes dizziness

*Why do you feel dizzy when you get out of bed - and what does it have to do with the fist clenched since birth.*

## Frozen Beast Pose

When any animal gets scared, the first thing it does is pull its head into its shoulders. Instinct. Protect your neck from a predator's bite. Have you ever been scared by a sharp sound? Remember - your shoulders fly up to your ears, your jaw clenches, your breath freezes. It happens in milliseconds, you don't even have control over it.

Now imagine that you live in this state. Not for a second. Not for a minute. **For months.**

Chronic stress keeps the body in constant microspasm. You don’t notice this, just like you don’t notice that you’re carrying a heavy bag - your hand dries up, but you don’t shift it. Because I'm used to it. The spasm became the norm.

In psychology there is a cruel but absolutely accurate phrase: **“If you can’t scream with your mouth, your body starts screaming”**. All suppressed emotions, everything that you cannot say out loud or express with action, does not evaporate. Stress energy simply hides deep in the body and comes out as a physical illness. Chronic stress always hits the weakest link of the body - where it is thin, it breaks:
* **Lump in the throat** is not a beautiful metaphor, but a literal conflict of the muscles of the larynx: they try to open for a scream and at the same time contract in order to hold back this scream.
* **Stomach problems** are a classic reflection of the inability to “digest” some difficult life situation.
* **Back pain** is often the same unbearable burden of hyper-responsibility.
* **Dizziness with PPPD** is a signal from an overloaded nervous system that cannot cope with an unresolved internal conflict.

Back in the 1930s, psychoanalyst Wilhelm Reich described this and called it **"muscular armor"**. He identified seven segments where the body blocks emotions:

1. **Eyes** - tense forehead, “frozen” eyes
2. **Mouth** - clenched teeth, clenched jaw
3. **Neck** - muscles like stone
4. **Chest** - shallow breathing, “lump in the chest”
5. **Diaphragm** - stiff, breathing does not reach the stomach
6. **Stomach** - spasm, irritable bowel syndrome
7. **Pelvis** - tension that you are not aware of at all

Every repressed emotion gets stuck in one of these blocks. You don't scream - your throat is blocked. You’re not angry out loud—your jaw clenches. You endure - your shoulders turn to stone.

## Why neck = dizziness

Now comes the most important part.

The neck muscles are not just muscles. They are densely packed with **proprioceptors** - tiny sensors that tell the brain where the head is. This is the third “balance sensor” of the three we talked about.

When the neck is in spasm, these sensors are clamped along with it. The signal from them goes to the brain with a **microdelay**.

You get out of bed in the morning. What's happening:

- **Eyes** see: you got up.
- **The gyroscope in the ear** senses: you are moving.
- **Clamped neck** says: “We are still lying!”

The brain interprets this desynchronization—in milliseconds—as a loss of balance. Dizziness. Unsteadiness. "Vata" in the head.

But there is a nuance that makes everything even worse. Anxiety turns you into an **iron bucket with an alarm clock inside**. A healthy person also experiences a slight sway when turning their head - this is normal vestibular physics. He just doesn't pay attention to it. And you, being in an anxiety disorder, literally listen to every micromovement. The alarm clock is the same as that of a healthy person. But you hear it 100 times louder, because the bucket is iron and the ringing is reflected from the walls. You turn your head 10 degrees: *“Oh, I’m dizzy!”*. What if it's 60? *“That’s it, it’s over, God forbid!”*. You are afraid of dizziness itself - and it is this fear that intensifies it. Vicious circle: a dog chasing its own tail.

And you run to the MRI in horror. And the MRI is clean. Because the problem is not the structure. **The problem is the signal.** More precisely, it’s how your frightened brain interprets a completely normal signal.

## Trapezoidal trigger

90% of people with PPPD have tender spots in the trapezius muscles. Press your fingers into the muscles between your neck and shoulders. Hurt? Here.

These points give **referred pain to the back of the head**, which doctors confuse with “impaired blood circulation in the brain.” They prescribe Cerebrolysin, Actovegin, Mexidol - dummy drugs that do not work. Because they are treating the wrong problem.

The problem is tight muscles. And the muscles are tense - because the brain is afraid.

## Temporary relief: massage and acupuncture

You go to a massage therapist or an acupuncture session (acupuncture, dry needling). The specialist kneads the neck or sticks needles into trigger points. At this moment a miracle happens. 

Physiologically it works like this:
1. **Local release:** The needle or strong pressure physically breaks the stagnant microspasm at the trigger point, restoring blood flow and flushing out accumulated waste products (lactic acid).
2. **Gate Control Theory:** A strong tactile or painful signal from a needle/massage travels to the spinal cord faster than a chronic background signal from a tight neck. The brain switches to a new stimulus and temporarily “forgets” to send the command to spasm.
3. **Endorphin Release:** Needles stimulate the release of natural analgesics, which provide a feeling of lightness and relaxation.

After the session you come out looking like new. The head is clear, the unsteadiness is gone. You rejoice: “This is it! The cause has been found, osteochondrosis has been defeated!”

But two days later, everything returns to normal. Sometimes it gets even worse.

Why? Because **the command to tense your muscles continues to come from your frightened processor (brain)**. You cleaned the local port, but the malicious alarm code continues to send endless packets of data to the spasm. It's like mopping up a puddle on the floor without turning off the tap. 

Does this mean that needles and massage are useless? No. They perfectly relieve the acute phase and help restore blood circulation in degraded muscles. But they work **only as a preparation for Jacobson’s relaxation and brain retraining**. If you do not reset the alarm generator itself, your visits to massage therapists will become an eternal subscription to temporary relief.

## Why does the shell come back after sleep

You noticed: in the morning the unsteadiness and stiffness of the neck is often worse than in the evening. It would seem that you lay there for 8 hours, resting, your muscles should relax. But the opposite happens. Why?

First of all, **sleeping during anxiety is not rest**. When you fall asleep with high cortisol levels, the brain does not fully enter deep sleep mode (N3/SWS phase). He is “half asleep” - vigilant. The muscles do not relax completely because the amygdala continues to send background signals: *“We are in danger, hold the line”*. You wake up with a “stone” neck, not because you slept uncomfortably, but because your brain kept your muscles on alert all night.

Secondly, **morning cortisol peak (CAR)**. In the first 30–45 minutes after waking up, cortisol levels physiologically soar by 50–75%. For a healthy person, this is simply a “power button” - the body prepares for an active day. But in a neurotic person, this surge falls on the already overheated limbic system, and the brain interprets it as an alarm signal. Hence the morning stiffness, the “wobbly” head and the instantaneous activation of the internal scanner.

**What to do:** Don’t lie in bed after waking up “listening to your body.” The first 60 seconds - get up, wash your face with cold water, take 5 slow belly breaths. This switches the brain from internal monitoring mode to external monitoring. Morning Jacobson (3-5 minutes) immediately after getting up is a powerful tool against the morning shell.

## Self-massage of trigger points of the neck and shoulders

If you pressed on the trapezius and felt pain, that means there are trigger points there. These are knots of muscle spasm that can persist for weeks or months, maintaining background unsteadiness. Here's how to work with them yourself:

### Point 1: Trapezius muscle (superior fascicle)
* **Where:** Muscle between the neck and shoulder, halfway.
* **How ​​to find:** With the opposite hand, feel the “mound” on the trapezium. Press. If you suddenly get sick, you found it.
* **What to do:** Use the ball of your thumb to apply moderate pressure (5-6 out of 10 on the pain scale, no more). Hold the pressure for 30-60 seconds until the pain begins to “spread” and decrease. Repeat 3 times on each side.

### Point 2: Suboccipital muscles
* **Where:** At the base of the skull, on either side of the spine.
* **How ​​to find:** Lie on your back. Place 2 tennis balls (or 2 walnuts in a sock) under the back of your head so that they press on the muscles on either side of the spinal column.
* **What to do:** Just lie on the balls for 3-5 minutes. Gravity does the work for you. If it hurts a lot, put a towel on it to soften it.

### Point 3: Sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM)
* **Where:** Lateral surface of the neck - the muscle goes from the ear to the collarbone. Feels like a dense cord.
* **How ​​to find:** Turn your head to the left - a “rope” will appear on the right side of your neck. This is GKSM.
* **What to do:** Gently grab the muscle with your thumb and forefinger. Gently “roll” it between your fingers from bottom to top. Don't press too hard - this is where the carotid arteries pass. 30 seconds on each side.

**Regularity:** Do self-massage every evening before Jacobson. In 2 weeks, the volume of muscle tension will decrease by 40–60%.

## Fascial chains: why do your legs hurt when your neck is tight?

You may have noticed that it's not just your neck that hurts. Pulls the lower back. The calves are buzzing. Feet feel like lead. And you think: “Well, that’s it, in addition to dizziness, my legs are also falling off. Probably multiple sclerosis!

Calmly. This is the **fascial chain** - and it explains everything.

Muscles in the body do not exist in isolation, like individual parts in a construction set. They are connected by **fascia** - a thin but incredibly strong connective tissue membrane that envelops each muscle and connects them in continuous chains from head to toe. Anatomist Thomas Myers called them **"anatomical trains"**.

The most important circuit for a PPPD practitioner is the **posterior superficial line**. It goes like this:

**Pole of the foot → calf muscle → hamstring → sacrum → erector spinae → occiput → aponeurosis (forehead fascia)**

This is one continuous “cable channel”. When the upper end (neck and back of the head) is locked in a chronic spasm, the tension is transmitted **down the entire chain**, as if you were pulling one end of a tight rope. Result: you feel “lead” in your legs, nagging pain in the lower back and stiffness in your feet - and all this is **from a tight neck**, and not from “sore legs”.

### Practice: roller myofascial release

Buy a foam roller or use a regular plastic water bottle (1.5 liters). Lie down on the roll and slowly roll the back line from bottom to top:

1. **Feet:** Place your foot on the roll, roll back and forth. 1 minute on each leg.
2. **Calves:** Sit on the floor and place the roll under your calves. Raise your pelvis and roll. 1 minute.
3. **Ham:** Roll under your hips, roll slowly. 1 minute.
4. **Lumbar and shoulder blades:** Lie with your back on the roll, hands behind your head, slowly roll from the lower back to the shoulder blades. 2 minutes.

**When:** In the evening, before self-massage of the neck and Jacobson. 5 minutes. After a week, you will notice that the “lead legs” have become lighter - because you have removed the tension from the entire chain, and not just from the top link.

## Diaphragmatic block: dizziness due to breathing

Wilhelm Reich identified 7 segments of the shell. We examined the neck (3rd segment) in detail. But there is another critically important one - **diaphragm** (5th segment).

The diaphragm is the muscular partition between the chest and abdomen. She is the main engine of breathing. When you're stressed, the diaphragm spasms and gets stuck in a half-retracted position. Breathing becomes **shallow and chesty**: the chest rises up and down, but the stomach does not move.

Here's what it does to your wobbliness:

1. **Hyperventilation.** Shallow breathing becomes more frequent. You're exhaling too much CO₂. The level of carbon dioxide in the blood drops.
2. **Respiratory alkalosis.** Blood pH shifts to the alkaline side. The blood vessels in the brain narrow (blood carries less oxygen to the brain).
3. **Result:** Dizziness, “fog” in the head, tingling in the fingers, feeling of “cotton legs”, ringing in the ears.

You run to a neurologist. He does an ultrasound scan. He says: “The blood vessels are normal.” And he’s right - the vessels are normal. But they are **functionally narrowed** because you are not breathing correctly. But you're breathing incorrectly because your diaphragm is tight. And she is clamped because her brain is afraid.

### Test: where do you breathe?

Sit up straight. Place **right palm on chest**, **left palm on stomach** (below navel). Take a normal breath. Don't try, just breathe as usual.

* If the **right** (chest) rises → the diaphragm is blocked. You breathe from the tops of your lungs.
* If the **left** (stomach) rises → the diaphragm works. You are breathing correctly.
* If both → you’re already doing great, but check if you’re forcing your breath.

### Practice: “Book on your stomach”

1. Lie on your back. Place a heavy book on your stomach (below your navel).
2. Inhale through your nose so that the book **rises**. The chest remains motionless.
3. Exhale through your mouth, the book **falls**. Exhale 2 times longer than inhalation (inhale 4 seconds → exhale 8 seconds).
4. 5 minutes, 2 times a day (morning and evening).

A book is visual feedback for the brain. You see your stomach rise → the brain remembers the pattern → after 2 weeks, diaphragmatic breathing becomes automatic. Hyperventilation stops. The “fog” in my head goes away.

## Jaw: bruxism and dizziness

The second segment of the shell according to Reich is **mouth and jaw**. And this is a blind spot that neither neurologists nor ENT specialists know about.

Chronic teeth clenching (bruxism) is a reaction to suppressed aggression. You can't scream → your jaw clenches. You endure → teeth clenched. You get angry in silence → your chewing muscles turn to stone. This happens during the day (micro-compression that you don’t notice) and at night during sleep.

Why is it associated with dizziness:

* **The temporomandibular joint (TMJ)** is located millimeters from the vestibular apparatus (inner ear). Chronic spasm of the masticatory muscles creates pressure on the joint, which is transmitted to the structures of the middle ear.
* **Referred pain.** Spasm of the masticatory muscles (especially the lateral pterygoid) produces pain that radiates to the temple, behind the ear and to the back of the head. Many people with PPPD have been going to the ENT for years with “ear pain”, but the problem is in the jaw.
* **Tinnitus (tinnitus).** A tight TMJ can cause clicking and buzzing in the ears, which a neurotic person immediately interprets as “Meniere’s disease” or “tumor of the auditory nerve.”

### Self-diagnosis of TMJ

1. Open your mouth as wide as possible. Do **3 fingers** (index, middle, ring) fit vertically? If not, the joint is limited.
2. Open your mouth slowly. Is there a **click** or crunch? Does the lower jaw move **to the side** when opening? These are signs of TMJ dysfunction.
3. Press your fingers on the point **in front of the tragus of the ear** (the depression in front of the auricle) with your mouth closed. Hurt? This is the joint capsule of the TMJ.

### Practice: Exercise “Fish”

1. Open your mouth, relax your lower jaw. Let it “hang” under its own weight.
2. Place the tip of your tongue on the roof of your mouth (behind your upper teeth).
3. Exhale slowly through your mouth. The jaw is as relaxed as possible, like a fish.
4. Hold for 30 seconds. Repeat 5 times.

**Bonus:** Before going to bed, place a warm compress (towel soaked in warm water) on both sides of your jaw for 3 minutes. This relieves nighttime bruxism and reduces morning stiffness. If bruxism is severe (teeth wear, morning jaw pain), contact your dentist for a custom-made mouthguard.

## Workplace: “text neck” and 8 hours of spasm

You do Jacobson morning and evening. Massage trigger points. And then you sit down at the computer for 8 hours with your neck stretched forward towards the monitor. And you wonder why the stone shoulders return in the evening.

This is called **forward head posture** - “text neck”. Every 2.5 centimeters of moving the head forward from the axis of the spine adds **5 kg of load** to the neck muscles. The average office worker's head is moved forward by 5–7 cm. This is 10–15 kg of constant load on the already tight trapezius. 8 hours a day. 5 days a week. No Jacobson can compensate for that.

### Workplace checklist (5 minutes once)

* **Monitor:** The top edge of the screen is at eye level. If you have a laptop, buy a stand (or place it on a stack of books) and an external keyboard.
* **Distance:** Extend your arm - your fingertips should touch the screen. No closer.
* **Chair:** The back is pressed against the backrest. Feet completely on the floor. Knees bent 90°.
* **Phone:** Don’t bow your head to the phone. Raise the phone to eye level (yes, your arms will get tired, but your neck will thank you).

### Microbreaks: every 45 minutes

Set a timer. Every 45 minutes, get up and do:

1. **Chin tuck:** While sitting, without tilting your head, move your chin back, as if you were giving yourself a “double chin.” Hold for 5 seconds, repeat 5 times. This stretches the suboccipital muscles and returns the head to its axis.
2. **Circular movements of the shoulders:** 5 forward, 5 backward. Slowly, with maximum amplitude.
3. **Inhale-exhale:** One deep breath in the stomach, long exhale.

15 seconds. But there are 10 such breaks per day. This is 150 seconds of targeted release of neck spasm that accumulates from the pose.

## Thermal contrast therapy for the neck

A simple but powerful tool for losing muscle mass every night. Alternating heat and cold on the neck and trapezius:

1. **Warm compress:** Wet a towel with hot water (not boiling water!), wring it out, wrap it around your neck and shoulders. Hold **3 minutes**. Heat dilates blood vessels, increases blood flow, and relaxes spasmodic fibers.
2. **Cool compress:** Replace with a towel soaked in cold water. Hold **1 minute**. Cold tones the walls of blood vessels and relieves swelling in inflamed trigger points.
3. **Repeat 3 cycles**, finish warm.

**When:** Every evening, **before** self-massage and Jacobson. Thermal contrast prepares the muscles - after it the massage goes deeper and relaxation is more effective. Total time - 12 minutes.
