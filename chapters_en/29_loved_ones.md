# Relatives and PPPD: instructions for those who are nearby 

*How PPPD changes a family, why a partner becomes a crutch or an executioner, and what to do to prevent the disease from devouring your relationship.*

---

## 1. Essence and issues: invisible illness at the center of the family 

PPPD is an invisible disorder. You don't lie with a cast. You don't have a fever. “I feel bad” is not written on my forehead. From the outside you look absolutely normal - and this is the main problem. 

Your loved ones are trapped. They see two different people: 

* **You say:** *“I’m shaking, I’m scared, I can’t leave the house.”* 
* **They see:** A person who walks, talks, eats normally and looks healthy. 

From this gap two typical scenarios are born, and both are destructive: 

**Scenario 1: “You’re just making it up!”** 
The partner does not believe. * “Stop beating yourself up. Go to work. All your tests are normal. Just stop thinking about it."* You feel misunderstood, alone, and begin to **exaggerate** symptoms so that you can finally be heard. Or you become isolated and stop telling - and the suppressed resentment goes into muscle spasm. 

**Scenario 2: “Poor thing, let me do everything for you”** 
The partner believes too much. He begins to protect you from everything: * “Don’t go to the store, I’ll go myself. Don't leave the house in case you feel bad. Let's cancel the trip."* Sound like a concern? In fact, it is a **disservice** that cements your neurosis. Your partner unknowingly becomes a **crutch**, without which you are afraid to take a step. And the exposition (Chapter 12) becomes impossible, because there is always a person nearby, ready to “save”. 

## 2. Physiological explanation: mirror neurons and emotional contagion 

Anxiety is not just **your** problem. It is literally **contagious** on a neurophysiological level. 

There are **mirror neurons** in the brain - cells that are activated not only when you yourself perform an action or experience an emotion, but also when you **observe** it in someone else. When your partner sees you having a panic attack, their amygdala fires in response. Not as strong as yours, but enough to trigger a cortisol release. 

What does this mean for the family:

* **Partner burnout.** Living next to a chronically anxious person is like living next to a working alarm system. The partner’s nervous system is also overloaded. After 6–12 months, the partner may develop his own symptoms: insomnia, irritability, headaches. 
* **Anxiety spiral.** You panic → your partner gets scared → you see his fear → you panic more → he gets even more scared. Two overheated brains throw firewood at each other. 
* **Role shift.** From a “partner”, a loved one turns into a “nurse” or “therapist”. Romance and intimacy are replaced by the function of “making sure he doesn’t get sick.” Relationships from horizontal (two equal adults) become vertical (one is caring, the other is sick). It kills both relationships and recovery. 

## 3. Four toxic patterns paired in PPPD 

### Pattern 1: “Crutch” 

Your partner does **everything** for you. You stop going to the store, driving a car, meeting friends - because * “why, if he/she can do it for me?”*. 

**Why it's dangerous:** Every time your partner “rescues” you from a situation, he confirms to your brain: *“The situation is really dangerous. You are right to be afraid.”* The brain writes: “Supermarket = danger (confirmed by partner’s behavior).” Avoidance intensifies. PPPD is cemented. 

### Pattern 2: “Executioner” 

The partner devalues. * “Stop whining. Pull yourself together. People have cancer, and here you are with your dizziness.”* 

**Why is this dangerous:** Devaluation is not motivation, it is **a blow to the nervous system**. You feel shame, guilt and loneliness. Suppressing your anger (Chapter 28). The muscle armor is strengthened. The instability is growing. 

### Pattern 3: “Therapist” 

The partner listens to a 2-hour report of symptoms every evening. *“And in the morning it was 7 out of 10, and then it became 5, and then I went into the elevator and it became 8...”* 

**Why it's dangerous:** You feed your CAS (Chapter 10). Every evening “debriefing” with a partner is the same rumination, only with an audience. Your partner gets tired, you get no relief, and both go to bed with elevated cortisol. 

### Pattern 4: “Hostage” 

The entire life of a family is built around the disease. Cancellation of trips. Refusal of guests. No restaurants, movies, travel - *“what if I feel bad”*. The partner and children live in the “tiptoe around the patient” mode.

**Why this is dangerous:** It enhances secondary gain (Chapter 17). The brain receives reinforcement: * “While I’m sick, the family adapts, I don’t need to make decisions, they don’t demand anything from me.”* Recovery is unconsciously sabotaged because it means returning to responsibility. 

## 4. Cognitive traps 

### Trap No. 1: “The partner must understand everything without words” 

*“If he/she loves me, he/she should feel how bad I feel, without explanation”*. 

Truth: your partner is not a telepath. He doesn't feel your dizziness. He doesn't know what PPPD is. He didn't read this book. If you don't explain, he won't understand. This doesn't mean he doesn't love you. This means that you have different information. 

### Trap #2: “I need to protect my loved ones from my problems” 

* “Why load children? Why spoil the mood of your husband/wife? I can handle it myself.”* 

Truth: if you are silent, your loved ones **still** feel the tension, but do not understand the reason for it. And they fill the void with fantasies: *“He/she stopped loving me”*, *“He’s hiding something”*, *“I’m probably to blame”*. Honest communication is better than anxious silence. 

### Trap #3: “I must fully recover before returning to a normal family life.” 

Truth: Recovery **occurs within** normal life, not instead of it. If you wait until you are 100% healthy to go to the park with your child, you will never go. Go now. With unsteadiness. With anxiety. This is the exposition. 

## 5. Point of Support: Insight 

> PPPD is not just your disease. This is a disease that runs in your family. If you only treat yourself, and the system of relationships remains the same, you both pedal in place. Recovery is a team game. But the rules of this game need to be **said out loud** - clearly, specifically, without guessing.

## 6. Practice 

### For you: “Five-minute briefing” instead of a two-hour complaint 

Replace the nightly endless analysis of symptoms with a **structured briefing** - 5 minutes, no more: 

1. **“Today there was [X] out of 10”** - one digit. Without details, without minute-by-minute analysis. 
2. **“I did [X]”** - what exactly did you do to recover (gymnastics, exposure, walk). 
3. **“I need [X] from you”** - a specific request. *"Please just hug me"* / *"I need to be alone for 30 minutes"* / *"Tomorrow we'll go to the store together, it will be easier for me if you're there for the first 10 minutes"*. 

All. 5 minutes. The partner is not overloaded. You don't feed CAS. Information has been transmitted. 

### For a partner: “Memo for those who are nearby” 

Pass this section on to your loved one. This is written **for him/her**:

---

**Dear loved one.** 

Your partner is experiencing PPPD - persistent postural-perceptual dizziness. It is a real neurological disorder recognized by WHO. This is not fiction, not hypochondria, and not “just nerves.” Your loved one's brain temporarily misprocesses signals from the body, causing them to feel unsteady, swaying, and anxious, even when standing on a level floor. 

**Here's what you need to know:** 

1. **It's not dangerous.** No one dies, faints, or has a stroke from it. It's unpleasant and scary - but not dangerous. 
2. **It is treatable.** PPPD is a completely reversible condition. Recovery takes 2–6 months with regular work. 
3. **You cannot cure him/her.** It is not your responsibility. You can **support**, but not **heal**. Treatment is his/her job. 

**What helps:** 

| ✅ Do it | ❌ Don't | 
|:---------|:----------| 
| Believe the symptoms are real | Saying “you're making this up” or “pull yourself together” | 
| Ask: “How can I help right now?” | Ask every 5 minutes “how are you?” | 
| Support the exhibition: “Let's go together, I'll be there” | Do EVERYTHING for him/her (go to the store, drive the car) | 
| Live your life: visit friends, do hobbies | Throwing away your life and becoming a caregiver | 
| Limit talking about illness to 5 minutes a day | Listen to two-hour monologues about symptoms every evening | 
| Say: “I believe in you. You can handle it" | Saying: “Maybe you shouldn’t go there?” | 
| Be patient: there will be good days and bad days | Angry that “I’ve been on treatment for a month now, but there’s no result” | 
| Take care of **yourself**: if you burn out, there will be no one to help | Ignore your condition for the sake of “caring” for your partner | 

**Most important:** The best thing you can do is **live normally**. Go to the movies, plan trips, invite guests. Do not adjust your family life to your illness. Normal life around the patient is a signal to his brain: * “The world is safe. Everything is going as usual. There is no need to panic."*

### For both of you: “The Contract for Recovery” 

Sit down together and agree on the rules. Write them down. This is not a formality - it is the **architecture** of your journey to recovery together. 

Example contract:

```
КОНТРАКТ НА ВЫЗДОРОВЛЕНИЕ

Date:___________ 

We, [Name 1] and [Name 2], agree: 

1. TIME TO BE SICK: We discuss symptoms and how you are feeling. 
Once a day, in the evening, no more than 5 minutes. 
Beyond this time we talk about life, not illness. 

2. EXPOSURE: [Name 2] supports exits to difficult places, 
but doesn't do EVERYTHING for [Name 1]. We are planning the stairs together 
fears and celebrate progress. 

3. CRUTCHES: [Name 1] agrees not to ask [Name 2] to perform 
tasks that he can perform independently (shopping, 
driving, making calls), even if it is alarming. 

4. SPACE: [Name 2] has the right to his life, friends 
and guilt-free hobbies. Partner burnout is the enemy 
recovery. 

5. STOP SIGNAL: If one of us feels that the pattern 
violated (crutch, executioner, therapist, hostage) - he says 
code word [_______], and we return to the contract. 

Signatures: ___________ / ___________ 
``` 

### How to talk to children about PPPV 

If you have children, they **already** feel that something is wrong. Children read their parents' anxiety instantly. If you are silent, the child fills the void with fantasies: *“Mom/Dad is sick with something terrible”*, *“It’s because of me”*, *“We’re all going to die”*. 

**What to say (adapt according to age):** 

* **3–6 years:** *“Mom/dad sometimes gets dizzy, like on a carousel. It's not scary and not contagious. The doctor knows and helps. You are not to blame for anything.”* 
* **7-12 years:** *“I have a thing called PPPD - my brain sometimes confuses the signals and it feels like the floor is shaking. It's like a bug in a computer - not dangerous, but unpleasant. I do special exercises to fix this. It will get better in a few months.”* 
* **Teenagers:** Can you let me read Chapter 1. A teenager is able to understand the mechanism. The main thing is to say: * “I'm working on it. I don't need your pity. I need your ordinary life - study, friends, music. The more normal you live, the better I feel.”*

1. **Send the “Memo for Someone Nearby” section to your partner/loved one.** If it’s awkward, say: *“I read a chapter in the book. There's a piece there just for you. Read it, please – it’s 3 minutes.”* 
2. **Complete the Recovery Contract with your partner.** Post it on the refrigerator or save it in your shared notes. Re-read once a month.

4. **Introduce the “Five Minute Briefing”** instead of nightly conversations about symptoms. After a week, ask your partner: *“Do you feel better?”*. Most likely yes. And you too.
