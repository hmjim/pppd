# Grieving Your Healthy Life: How to Mourn the Person You Used to Be

## The Photo on the Desktop

You have a photo. Maybe on your phone. Maybe in your head. In it — the old you. Running a marathon. Dancing at a friend's wedding. Carrying your kid on your shoulders down a mountain trail. The you who didn't count steps to the nearest wall, didn't check whether the restaurant had chairs with backs, didn't wake up thinking "how bad will the swaying be today."

You look at that photo and feel a hole in your chest. Because that person is dead. Not literally. But the version of you that lived without thinking about balance — it's gone. And nobody came to the funeral.

The strangest part is that you can't explain this grief to the people around you. "You're alive! Arms, legs, all there! What's there to grieve?" And yet you grieve. And you feel guilty for grieving. A double blow.

I remember finding a video of me and my friends playing volleyball on the beach. Jumping, yelling, falling in the sand, laughing. I watched it and cried. Not from pain — from longing. As if I'd lost someone close. And I really had — my former self.

## Grief Isn't About Weakness — It's a System Process

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross described five stages of grief. They're usually applied to the death of a loved one. But they work exactly the same when you lose your health. Because to the brain, a loss is a loss, no matter what: a person, a job, or the ability to walk without dizziness.

**Denial.** "This will pass. It's temporary. One more MRI and they'll find the cause." You see ten doctors because the eleventh will surely say something different. You google symptoms until three in the morning, searching for a diagnosis that can be cured with a pill.

**Anger.** "Why me?! I lived a healthy lifestyle! My neighbor smokes, drinks, eats garbage — and he's healthy as a horse!" Rage at your body, at doctors, at the universe. Fair, hot, helpless rage.

**Bargaining.** "If I exercise every day, maybe it'll let up?" "Maybe if I move to another city, change the climate…" "If only I hadn't been so stressed at work back then, none of this would've happened." Endless "what ifs" that burn like acid.

**Depression.** Silence. A gray world. "What's the point of trying if it's never going to get better." You lie there staring at the ceiling. Not because you're tired — because you can't see a reason to get up.

**Acceptance.** No, this isn't "hooray, I've made peace with it." It's a quiet, sometimes painful understanding: "This version of the operating system is no longer supported. Time to install a new one."

These stages don't go in order. You can get stuck in anger for six months, jump to acceptance for a week, and crash back into denial after the next flare-up. That's not a setback. That's a normal reboot process. The system is trying different configurations until it finds a stable one.

## The "Things Were Better Before" Trap

The most toxic myth of grief is that acceptance means giving up. That if you stop fighting reality, you've lost. That's a lie.

Acceptance isn't a white flag. It's a reallocation of resources. As long as you're at war with what already happened, you have no energy left to change what's happening now. Like a developer who spends all their time complaining that the client wrote a garbage spec instead of actually writing code.

There's another version of this trap — "idealizing the past." You remember your healthy self as a person without problems. But honestly: before PPPD, things bothered you too. It's just that against the backdrop of dizziness, everything else looks like paradise. You're comparing the present not to your real past, but to a Photoshopped version of it.

The second trap is "anniversary reactions." Many people notice: every year, around the month their dizziness started, symptoms flare up. You're not losing your mind. It's implicit memory. The brain remembers the date even if you've forgotten. And it re-triggers the stress response. Know about this — and the flare-up stops being a catastrophe. It's just an echo request from an old system.

## Healthy Grief vs. Stuck Grief

Healthy grief is when you feel sad, cry, get angry — and gradually start living again. It comes in waves: it hits and it recedes. Each time the waves get quieter.

You might feel fine for a week and then see someone jogging in the park — and something clenches inside. That's a normal wave. It will pass. Don't cling to it and don't be scared of it. The system is processing the loss file. Give it time.

Stuck grief is when, three years in, every evening you're scrolling through photos of "healthy you" and thinking: "I want to go back." When you greet any improvement not with joy but with the thought: "But it's not what it used to be." When the new "good" never measures up to the old one.

If you recognized yourself in the second scenario — it's not a death sentence. It means the system needs help with garbage collection: objects have piled up in memory that need to be released. Psychotherapy is the best tool for that. Not "talking to a friend." Not "pulling yourself together." Therapy.

A hallmark of stuck grief is the phrase "I'll never be the same." And you know what? That's true. You won't be the same. But that's not the end of the sentence. The full version goes: "I'll never be the same — I'll be different." Different doesn't mean worse. Different means different.

## You're Not Who You Were. You're More

Here's what I understood after years: I didn't just lose something. I gained something. Empathy for others' pain that I never had before. Depth that doesn't emerge in comfort. The ability to notice the little things — because when the world is swaying, you learn to hold on to details.

As they say: "Every cloud has a silver lining." It sounds like mockery when you're in the storm. But once it lets up — you'll understand.

I used to run marathons. Now I walk through the park and feel every step as a victory. And you know what? That victory is worth more than any medal at the finish line. Because thousands ran the marathon. But what you've been through — only a few have survived that.

The Japanese have kintsugi — the art of repairing broken ceramics with gold. A broken cup mended with golden seams is worth more than an intact one. Because it holds a story. Your cracks aren't ugliness. They're golden seams that just aren't visible yet. But they're already there.

## Homework: The "Before and After" Letter

Sit down. Take two sheets of paper.

**Sheet 1: "What I Lost."** Write honestly. "I lost the ability to drive without fear. I lost lightness. I lost confidence. I lost spontaneity." Don't hold back tears if they come. That's not weakness — it's the system draining. Write on paper, not on your phone — the hand remembers better than the keyboard.

**Sheet 2: "What I Gained or Learned."** This one's harder. Maybe you learned to ask for help. Maybe you found out who your real friends are. Maybe you became more attentive to your body. Maybe you started treasuring an ordinary day without severe dizziness as a gift. Maybe for the first time you began taking care of your health consciously. Look for it. It's there, even if you can't believe it right now.

Give yourself a week. Carry these sheets with you and keep adding to them. Then re-read both. The first is the price. The second is what you bought with that price. And you decide: was it worth it.

If the second sheet is still blank — don't beat yourself up. It means you're still in the grieving process. Come back to it in a month. In two. The empty lines aren't a verdict. They're space for text that hasn't been written yet.

One condition: the second sheet must have at least as many items as the first. Not because you're obligated to be grateful. But because if you dig honestly — there really are that many.
