# The Shame of Illness: Why You Hide Your Dizziness

## The Invisible Wound

You're standing at a company party, holding a glass of juice, smiling. Inside, everything is swimming, your legs are like jelly, the floor is dropping out from under you. But you smile. Because if you tell the truth, you'll get: "Come on, you look great!"

You know what's worse than a chronic illness? A chronic illness nobody believes in. You're not in a cast. You don't have a scar. Your labs are "normal." Your MRI is clean. And you start feeling like an impostor of your own suffering.

For three years I hid from colleagues that I could barely stand. I went to meetings gripping the edge of the table until my knuckles turned white. I skipped lunches because the cafeteria didn't have a wall to lean against. And every evening at home I collapsed on the couch thinking: "Another day of pretending — survived."

You know what psychologists call this? Emotional labor. You're working two shifts: your actual job plus a full-time role in the movie "I'm Totally Fine." And nobody pays you for the second one.

## A Bug That Devours All Resources

Shame isn't just an emotion. It's a background process devouring 80% of your psyche's RAM. Neuroscientists at the University of Amsterdam have shown that shame activates the anterior cingulate cortex — the same brain region that processes physical pain. Your brain literally can't tell the difference between hitting your finger with a hammer and hearing "you're just being lazy."

It gets worse. Shame triggers a cortisol spike. Cortisol heightens the sensory sensitivity of the vestibular system. PPPD symptoms ramp up. You feel even worse — and the shame deepens. A closed loop, infinite recursion with no exit condition.

For men, multiply that by two. "A man should tough it out." "Stop whining like a baby." "Get to work, quit making stuff up." I heard this from my father, from friends, from the voice inside my head. And every one of those "shoulds" is another turn of the recursion.

For women, shame takes a different form, but it's no less destructive. "What kind of mother are you if you can't pick your kid up from school without panicking?" "Your husband is tired of your complaining — he'll leave soon." Shame strikes right at the sorest spot — at who you believe yourself to be.

## Social Isolation: Quarantine Mode

Shame does a terrible thing — it isolates you. Not physically. Emotionally. You stop calling friends because you don't know what to say. "How are you?" — "Same as always, still dizzy." It gets old for you and for them.

You start declining invitations. First the loud parties — too many triggers. Then the quiet get-togethers — what if you feel awful in front of people. Then any plans at all — easier to lie and say you're busy than to explain.

I got to the point where I only talked to my wife and the delivery driver who brought food. Two people in my entire ecosystem. A system running on the bare minimum of connections — like a server that's closed all its ports. Safe? Sure. Functional? No.

Isolation feels like protection, but it's a trap. The less you socialize, the scarier it is to step into the world. The scarier it gets, the deeper the shame. Yet another closed loop.

## Three Traps of Shame

**Trap #1: "If my tests are normal, I'm healthy — which means I'm just weak."** No. PPPD is a functional disorder. It doesn't show up on an MRI, just like a bug in code doesn't show up in a photograph of the monitor. That doesn't mean the bug isn't there.

**Trap #2: "I'm ashamed to ask for help because other people have it worse."** Imagine: your leg is broken, but you don't go to the doctor because someone out there has had an amputation. Absurd? Absurd. But that's exactly how you reason about your dizziness.

**Trap #3: "If I tell people, they'll pity me, and pity is humiliating."** Pity and compassion are different things. Pity looks down on you. Compassion meets you as an equal. And you have every right to the latter.

**Trap #4: "Other people with conditions like this manage to work, and I can't — so I must just be lazy."** Those "other people" have a different sensitivity threshold, a different nervous system, a different history. Comparing yourself to them is like comparing the load on two servers with different hardware. Pointless and harmful.

## Vulnerability Is Not Weakness — It's an Upgrade

There's an old saying: "What doesn't come to light can't be set right." But I'll put it more bluntly: what hides in the dark gets eaten alive. Shame only works in darkness. Like mold. Drag it into the light and it loses its power.

I remember the moment I first told a friend: "Listen, I have chronic dizziness. It's really hard for me." I braced for mockery. What I got was: "Dude, I thought you'd just gotten too full of yourself and stopped hanging out with us." Three years of shame — over a story I'd made up in my own head.

Brené Brown, a shame researcher at the University of Houston, calls this "vulnerability as strength." Not because showing weakness is easy. But because it takes more courage than pretending.

Think about it: it takes more bravery to say "I'm struggling" than to silently clench your teeth. Silence is autopilot. Honesty is manual control. And yes, manual control is harder. But it's the only way you'll take back control of the route.

## Shame Is Contagious — But So Is Courage

When you stop hiding, a strange thing happens: other people start telling the truth too. A colleague admits they have panic attacks. A neighbor opens up about their depression. You accidentally create a space where people can be real.

This doesn't mean telling every stranger you meet. It means stopping the waste of energy on camouflage. Every time you pretend to be healthy, you hand your symptoms the resources you could be spending on recovery. The system isn't overloaded by the illness — it's overloaded by concealing it.

Start with one person. Someone you trust. You don't have to reveal everything. Just: "I have a health issue that few people know about. I don't need pity. I just need someone to know." One person who knows the truth is a Point of Support from which you can topple all the shame.

## Homework: Shame Inventory

Take a sheet of paper. Divide it into three columns.

**Column 1: Situation.** Write down every moment where you feel shame because of PPPD. "Turned down a trip with friends." "Can't play soccer with my kid." "Sitting at home while my spouse works." Everything. No censoring.

**Column 2: What I tell myself.** Write down the inner voice. "I'm worthless." "Normal people don't live like this." "I'm a burden."

**Column 3: What I'd say to a friend.** Imagine a close friend told you all of this. What would you say back? Write it down. Word for word.

Then re-read the third column. Out loud. That is the truth. The first two are a bug in the system. The third is the patch.

At least 10 situations. Not five. Ten. Shame fears specificity — when you call it by name, it deflates like a balloon.
