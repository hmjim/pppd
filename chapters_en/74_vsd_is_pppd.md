# You Were Told "VSD"? Here's What's Actually Happening to Your Body

*Dizziness. Tachycardia. A lump in your throat. Unsteady when walking. Numb hands. Head like it's in a fog. The neurologist listened, looked at the tests — everything's normal. Diagnosis: "vegetovascular dystonia." Except this diagnosis doesn't exist.*

---

## VSD — A Diagnosis That Doesn't Exist

Vegetovascular dystonia (VSD) is **not a disease**. It's not in the ICD-10. Not in the ICD-11. Not in any international classifier. It's a Soviet-era artifact — a junk drawer where doctors dump everything they can't explain in a 12-minute appointment. This diagnosis is only used in Russian-speaking countries and has no international recognition.

When a neurologist says "VSD" — they're saying: "Something's going on with your autonomic nervous system, but I don't know what exactly, and I don't have time to figure it out." After that, you're prescribed Mexidol, Actovegin, Cavinton — drugs with **zero evidence base** — and sent home.

You take the pills. Nothing changes. You go to another neurologist. They say: "Neurocirculatory dystonia" (yet another empty label). They prescribe magnesium and nootropics. Six months pass. A year. Three years. You're still swaying.

---

## What's Actually Hiding Behind "VSD"

Behind the label "VSD" is usually one of the following:

**1. PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness).** If you're chronically unsteady, the world is floating, it gets worse in stores and while walking, and all tests are normal — this is not "dystonia." This is PPPD. ICD-11 code: AB32.0. A specific diagnosis with specific Bárány Society criteria and a specific treatment.

**2. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD).** If the main issue is constant anxiety, tension, inability to relax, insomnia, and the physical symptoms (tachycardia, tremor, sweating) are the consequence. ICD-10: F41.1.

**3. Panic Disorder.** If you have attacks: sudden pounding heart, shortness of breath, fear of death — and then it passes. ICD-10: F41.0.

**4. Somatic Symptom Disorder.** Multiple physical symptoms without an organic cause: pain, numbness, a lump in the throat, digestive issues. ICD-10: F45.

All of these diagnoses are **real, recognized by the WHO, with evidence-based treatments.** Unlike "VSD," which means nothing.

---

## Why This Matters: The Diagnosis Determines the Treatment

As long as you're being "treated for VSD" — you're being treated for **nothing**. Mexidol "for VSD" is like an antivirus for a nonexistent virus. You cycle through pills, go to chiropractors, get neck massages — and nothing helps. Because the problem isn't in your neck, not in your blood vessels, and not in some "tone imbalance."

The problem is in a nervous system that's stuck in anxiety mode. The amygdala thinks you're in danger. The body generates adrenaline. Muscles are in spasm. The vestibular system receives distorted signals. You sway, and get scared, and sway even more.

This is treatable. Not with Mexidol. With vestibular rehabilitation, CBT, exposure therapy, muscle relaxation, and — in severe cases — SSRIs. All of this is described in the book "Point of Support" — a step-by-step recovery system.

## What to Tell Your Neurologist

If you want to get proper help instead of another prescription for nootropics:

1. **Ask for a referral to an otoneurologist or vestibulologist.** Not a regular neurologist — a dizziness specialist.
2. **Name the diagnosis:** "I think I have PPPD — Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness, ICD-11 code AB32.0. My symptoms match the Bárány Society criteria."
3. **Ask them to consider SSRIs** (sertraline or escitalopram) — these are the only medications with proven effectiveness for PPPD. Not instead of doing the work on yourself — alongside it.
4. **Decline Mexidol, Actovegin, Cavinton, Cerebrolysin.** Politely but firmly. These drugs have no evidence base for functional dizziness.
