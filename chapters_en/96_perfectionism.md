# Perfectionism and PPPD: How Striving for Perfection Wrecks Your Vestibular System

*If you opened this chapter, chances are you’re used to carrying everything on your shoulders, never making mistakes, and keeping your standards sky-high—even when the ground is literally shifting beneath your feet. Welcome to the overachievers’ club, where chasing flawlessness lands you with chronic dizziness. Let’s figure out how to step down from that pedestal and finally start recovering.*

## 1. The Problem

Nearly one in two people who show up with PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness) used to be that "easygoing, dependable, flawless" person before symptoms hit—the one who beats every deadline, keeps everything spotless, and never complains. Perfectionism used to feel like your superpower. Then, at some point, that superpower overheated your system and became the main engine behind your unsteadiness.

Psychologists break perfectionism down into **three types** (the Hewitt and Flett model), and it helps to see how each one works against you:

- **Self-oriented perfectionism.** Your inner taskmaster with a whip. You set an impossibly high bar and tear yourself apart over the slightest slip. Miss a workout, stumble on a Zoom call, leave one item unchecked on your to-do list—and an internal tribunal instantly convenes.
- **Other-oriented perfectionism.** Demanding perfection from the people around you—your partner, coworkers, doctors. When the world falls short of your standards, you’re hit with a simmering frustration and the sense that you can’t rely on anyone.
- **Socially prescribed perfectionism.** The deep-seated belief that other people expect perfection from you and won’t forgive any sign of weakness. "I can’t let anyone see I’m unwell." "If I sway in public, they’ll think I’m drunk or losing it." "I have to keep it together at all costs."

Which one feeds PPPD the most? The primary fuel for your symptoms is the toxic duo of **socially prescribed** and **self-oriented** perfectionism. The first forces you to slap on an "everything’s fine" mask and burn massive amounts of energy hiding your dizziness. The second finishes the job from the inside: you resent your own body for "breaking down" and failing to deliver its usual 100% output.

The cruelest twist happens when you decide to get better. A perfectionist doesn’t know how to simply recover—you turn healing into an Olympic event where nothing less than gold will do. And as long as you’re trying to be the model patient, PPPD feels right at home.

## 2. The Neuroscience

To see how an overachiever’s mindset turns into a vestibular misfire, let’s look at the biology. To the ancient parts of your brain, there isn’t much difference between "a predator is charging at me" and "I didn’t nail that task—people are going to see my weakness." Either way, it flips the switch on a **chronic stress response**.

When you live under the constant pressure of "it has to be flawless," your **amygdala runs 24/7**. This threat center never gets the all-clear signal. Most people finish a task, exhale, and let the tension drop. For a perfectionist, "good enough" doesn’t exist: the second one task is done, your brain is already scanning for flaws or winding itself up for the next one.

Because of that, **cortisol stays elevated** for months on end. Your sympathetic nervous system gets stuck in high gear, and your **autonomic nervous system** keeps your body permanently braced for combat. Shallow breathing, deep muscular tension, and **muscle armor** create a hair-trigger baseline where your brain interprets even the tiniest wobble in balance as an emergency.

The core glitch happens in the cortex. To micromanage every detail of your life without a single slip, your **prefrontal cortex** runs in constant overdrive. Once it overheats, that center of conscious control barges into territory it was never meant to run—the autopilot behind your gait and balance.

Normally, you don’t think about how to place one foot in front of the other or what angle to hold your head—subcortical circuits handle all of that in milliseconds. But a perfectionist’s brain is used to driving everything on manual. It wrestles the steering wheel away from autopilot and jams a finely tuned algorithm. That’s how the **adrenaline loop** locks in: performance anxiety creates tension and unsteadiness, unsteadiness spooks the amygdala, cortisol spikes again, and you stay stuck in PPPD.

## 3. Cognitive Traps

Perfectionism is a master of disguise—it loves dressing up as discipline and common sense. When you’re dealing with PPPD, it sets three traps that almost every conscientious person walks straight into.

### Trap 1. Perfectionism in Recovery
"I’m doing my exercises flawlessly, down to the exact minute, tracking my breathing, and logging everything in a journal—and I’m still not better! Why isn’t this working?" Classic. The answer is simple: you’re not healing; you’re taking an exam.

When you turn **Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT)**, **CBT**, or simply leaving the house into an A+ performance review, your nervous system stays on high alert. You start demanding a payout for your diligence: "I’ve ground through this for two weeks straight without missing a day—where’s my clear head?!" But your brain doesn’t respond to being bullied by spreadsheets and schedules. As long as an inner taskmaster with a stopwatch is grading every step, your amygdala receives one clear message: "We’re in danger; we have to defeat this symptom right now." And the symptoms aren’t going anywhere.

### Trap 2. The Control Paradox and the Link to CAS
In **MCT (Metacognitive Therapy)**, the main enemy is **CAS (Cognitive Attentional Syndrome)**: endless symptom scanning, anxious rumination, and constant self-checking. Perfectionism and CAS are joined at the hip.

The harder you try to micromanage your body, the worse your symptoms get. Trying to walk "straight as an arrow," locking your gaze onto the horizon (**visual dependence**), and stiffening your neck are disguised **safety crutches**—and pure rocket fuel for CAS. Try walking down a flight of stairs while consciously calculating the exact angle of your knee bend on every step; within five steps, you’ll trip. You’re sabotaging your own balance the exact same way when you try to control it second by second.

### Trap 3. The All-or-Nothing Progress Scale
To an overachiever, there are only two outcomes: "100% healed with zero discomfort" or "total failure." The moment a normal **setback** hits after a good week—maybe from poor sleep or a shift in the weather—the perfectionist scratches out all their progress. Yet a setback is just a normal wave of brain recalibration, not a failing grade on your report card.

## 4. The Turning Point

Let’s be blunt with each other. Your perfectionism didn’t save you. It didn’t buy you safety, and it didn’t make you bulletproof—it just burned through your nervous system for years until your body finally pulled the emergency brake with PPPD.

The turning point in recovery comes when you reclaim your **right to be imperfect**. A living human being isn’t a robot balanced on gyroscopes. You can feel a brief sway when you stand up too fast, get a fuzzy head after a long day, blank on a word, show up late, and phone in your exercises at a C-minus.

Steadiness comes back the exact moment you stop white-knuckling for it. Take yourself down from the honor roll. You don’t need to ace your recovery—aim for a solid C, a little messy, without panic, and with room to mess up. Once your amygdala learns that a wobble won’t be followed by an internal firing squad, it will finally take its finger off the panic button.

## 5. Practice

Enough theory—let’s break the control habit through action. Doing these exercises "perfectly" is strictly off-limits: their entire value lies in being loose, messy, and real.

### Exercise 1. The "Deliberately Bad" Graded Exposure
This is direct **graded exposure** to your fear of imperfection. The goal is to prove to your amygdala in real life that a 60% effort is completely safe.

- **Step 1.** First thing in the morning, pick one everyday task where you’d normally go all out (a work email, tidying up, a report, a household chore). Set out to do it deliberately badly—at about a C-level.
- **Step 2.** Do it without double-checking or polishing. Send the email on the first draft, leave a couple of mugs in the sink, or step outside in an outfit that isn’t put together.
- **Step 3.** Notice the itch to "fix it—what will people think?" Don’t scratch it. Watch how your autonomic nervous system reacts, and tell yourself: "I did this imperfectly. My body isn’t used to it, but nothing catastrophic is happening."
- **Step 4.** That evening, check the evidence: did the sky fall? In 99% of cases, nobody even notices your "sloppy" job—and your brain logs a fresh experience of safety.

### Exercise 2. "Sloppy" Vestibular Rehabilitation
The goal is to pry VRT and your daily walks out of your perfectionist’s grip.

- **Step 1.** Do today’s short **Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT)** session with no timer, no counting reps, and no expectation of instant relief.
- **Step 2.** If you sway a little harder than usual, forbid yourself from hunting for what you did wrong ("I turned my head wrong," "great, another setback"). Say out loud: "Good—my brain just got a messy signal. Let it learn."
- **Step 3.** The second you finish, shift your attention to something outside yourself and go 20 minutes without checking in on how your head feels—starving CAS of its fuel.

### Exercise 3. Dropping Micro-Control on the Move
The goal is to hand walking back over to autopilot.

- **Step 1.** When you’re out walking, catch the moment you lock your eyes onto one fixed point, hike your shoulders up, and march in a rigid straight line to hide your PPPD.
- **Step 2.** Deliberately loosen up: drop your shoulders, unglue your eyes from that anchor point, and let your arms and torso swing lazily with your stride.
- **Step 3.** Walk like that for 3 minutes, refusing to check "how do I look right now?" Show your brain that an imperfect, slightly loose-limbed gait doesn’t make you fall.

## Homework

For the next 7 days, strike the word "flawless" from your vocabulary. Gather real-world proof that being imperfect is safe:

1. **The "Deliberately Bad" rule, every day.** Complete at least 7 small tasks over the week at 60–70% quality. Jot down a single line for each: what you left unpolished, and whether disaster struck.
2. **Symptom-journal amnesty.** Ditch the 1-to-10 scales and daily symptom ratings. Measure your day with one question: "Did I live my life today despite the unsteadiness, or did I wait around for a perfect day again?" (Yes / No).
3. **Three outings without armor.** At least three times this week, drop the Iron Man act: tell someone close to you that you’re tired, sit down on a bench to catch a breather, or ask for a simple favor without over-apologizing.
