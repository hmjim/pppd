What is PPPD?

PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness) is a chronic functional neuro-vestibular disorder in which an individual constantly feels unsteadiness, swaying, rocking, and dizziness, even though all medical investigations (cranial MRI, CT, blood work, audiometry) return completely normal. PPPD is officially indexed in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11, code AB32.0) and recognized by the Barany Society — the global gold standard in neuro-otology.

PPPD is not a psychiatric illness and not "in your head." It is a measurable neuro-sensory processing mismatch where the brain becomes stuck in a high-threat alarm state following an acute vestibular trigger (BPPV, vestibular neuritis, panic attack, intense burnout). The brain continues hyper-scanning for threat even after the initial physical event has long resolved.

According to epidemiological studies, PPPD is diagnosed in 15–20% of all patients visiting specialized balance clinics and is the second most common cause of neuro-otology visits worldwide after BPPV.

Symptoms of PPPD

Characteristic symptoms that distinguish PPPD from other vestibular disorders:

  • Unsteadiness and floating sensations — feeling like you are swaying, walking on a boat deck, or standing on a sponge floor
  • Worse in upright posture — symptoms intensify when standing or walking, but ease when lying down flat
  • Visual motion hypersensitivity — supermarkets, moving traffic, action movies, and fluorescent lighting trigger severe visual vertigo
  • Duration >3 months — symptoms present on most days for at least 3 consecutive months
  • Autonomic anxiety and body hyper-scanning — constant monitoring of balance signals and dread of collapsing
  • Suboccipital muscle armor — chronic clamping in the neck, shoulders, and jaw (muscle armor)
  • Derealization — feeling detached from reality, experiencing brain fog or seeing through glass
  • Normal diagnostic findings — clean MRI, CT, EKG, and blood test results

Causes of PPPD

PPPD develops through a cyclic neuro-somatic loop: "Anxiety → Muscle Clamping → Distorted Proprioceptive Feedback → Heightened Dizziness → More Anxiety". Common triggers include:

  • BPPV (Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo) — crystals resolved, but the brain retained the trauma pattern
  • Vestibular Neuritis — viral nerve inflammation after which central compensation failed to reset
  • Panic Attack — acute sympathetic surge triggering an emergency balance clamp
  • Chronic Stress & Burnout — prolonged nervous system overload without autonomic rest
  • Mild Concussion or Whiplash — post-concussive sensory mismatch evolving into chronic disequilibrium

PPPD Treatment Protocol

PPPD is fully treatable and reversible. The international medical consensus (Barany Society) recommends a comprehensive multi-modal approach:

  1. Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT) — structured exercise complex to recalibrate the Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR), Romberg balance drills, and optokinetic habituation.
  2. Cognitive Behavioral & Metacognitive Therapy (CBT/MCT) — dismantling cognitive distortions, catastrophic appraisals, and Cognitive Attentional Syndrome (CAS).
  3. Graded In-Vivo Exposure — systematic, controlled habituation to trigger environments (supermarkets, crowded spaces, driving).
  4. Physical Activity & Somatic Release — aerobic walking 30 minutes daily to stimulate BDNF, combined with Jacobson Progressive Muscle Relaxation.
  5. Pharmacotherapy — SSRIs/SNRIs (sertraline, escitalopram, venlafaxine) as central neuromodulators when indicated. Benzodiazepines are not recommended as they impair neuroplastic compensation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is PPPD?
PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness) is a functional neuro-vestibular disorder. A person feels chronic unsteadiness, floating, and disequilibrium despite all diagnostic scans showing normal results. It is the second most common cause of dizziness clinic consultations worldwide, diagnosed in 15–20% of vestibular patients. Read more about PPPD →
Can PPPD be fully cured?
Yes, PPPD is completely treatable. Primary methods: vestibular rehabilitation, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), graded exposure, and regular physical activity. The process takes 2 to 6 months of daily protocol practice. The author fully recovered within 6 months. Read recovery story →
How is PPPD different from BPPV?
BPPV causes brief attacks of violent rotational vertigo upon head position changes due to displaced inner ear otoliths. PPPD is a chronic, background unsteadiness that frequently develops AFTER BPPV. Learn more →
Which exercises help with PPPD?
Vestibular rehabilitation exercises help: eye movements (VOR x1/x2), head turns, and balance retraining. Jacobson Progressive Muscle Relaxation and daily walking are also highly effective.
What diagnostic workup is required?
Brain MRI, carotid/vertebral Doppler, neurotology and neurology evaluations to rule out structural pathology. A complete checklist is provided in chapter “Closing the Clinic Door”.
How long does treatment take?
With daily commitment, noticeable relief begins in 4–8 weeks. Full recovery takes 2 to 6 months. Avoiding premature cessation of exercises is critical to prevent setbacks.
Is PPPD dangerous?
No. PPPD is a functional software disorder, not structural brain damage. It never causes strokes, paralysis, syncope, or fatal harm. Its main danger is reduced quality of life, which is why active rehabilitation is essential.
Can PPPD resolve on its own?
In rare mild cases, yes. But in most instances without structured re-education, PPPD chronifies for years because the brain gets locked in an alarm loop. Early rehabilitation is crucial.
Is derealization related to PPPD?
Yes. Derealization (feeling detached from reality or seeing through glass) is a common co-symptom. It is a protective autonomic fuse that activates when the sensory system is overwhelmed by anxiety. It is not psychosis.
Which medications help in PPPD?
Evidence-based medicine recommends SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram) or SNRIs (venlafaxine) as central neuromodulators. Old "vestibular suppressants" (betahistine, cinnarizine) are ineffective in PPPD. Benzodiazepines should be avoided as they inhibit central vestibular compensation.
