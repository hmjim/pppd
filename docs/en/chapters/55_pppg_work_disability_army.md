# PPPD and Life: Work, Sick Leave, Driving, and Military Service

*PPPD isn't just dizziness. It's also the questions you can't wave away: "Can I work? Can I get time off? Can I drive? What about military service?" Let's sort this out.*

## Work

I know what it's like. You're sitting at the computer — the screen is swimming. You stand up — swaying. Colleagues ask "why do you look so pale?" and you don't know how to explain that the floor under your feet feels like the deck of a ship in a storm.

### What Makes It Worse at Work

- **Screens** — scrolling through spreadsheets, monitor flickering, switching between tabs. [Visual dependence](08_visual_dependence.html) makes this torture.
- **Fluorescent lighting** — flickering tubes in the open-plan office. The flicker frequency is invisible to the eye, but the vestibular system picks it up and panics.
- **Cognitive fog** — [Brain fog](33_brain_fog.html). You read a paragraph three times and have no idea what it says. Not because you're stupid — because your brain is spending 80% of its resources maintaining balance. That leaves 20% for thinking.
- **Being sedentary** — sitting motionless for hours. The [muscle armor](04_muscle_armor.html) clenches your neck. Neck tension → distorted proprioception → unsteadiness.

### What Helps

- **Breaks every hour.** 5 minutes: stand up, walk to the window, look into the distance. Take 3 deep breaths. Roll your shoulders. Go back.
- **An external monitor at eye level.** Not a laptop down low that crunches your neck. A big screen at arm's length, top edge at eye level.
- **Box breathing** — inhale 4 sec, hold 4 sec, exhale 4 sec, pause 4 sec. Lowers your baseline cortisol right at your desk.
- **Don't hide it.** If you can — explain to your manager that you have a vestibular disorder. This isn't "being crazy," it's a medical diagnosis with code AB32.0 in [ICD-11](38_pppg_mkb.html).

### Should You Quit Your Job?

No. Unless you work at heights, on a construction site, or operate heavy machinery. Office work — you can manage. And staying home with PPPD is a trap: the less you move and go out, the more the brain "cements" the symptoms.

Work is exposure. Commuting to work is exposure. Talking to colleagues is exposure. All of it trains the brain that life is safe.

## Sick Leave

Yes, you can get sick leave for PPPD. Your neurologist can issue a temporary disability certificate during a flare-up.

### What Diagnosis Will They Write

Many healthcare systems still use ICD-10, which doesn't have a specific code for PPPD. So doctors use stand-in diagnoses:

| ICD-10 Code | What They'll Write |
|------------|-------------------|
| **H81.9** | Unspecified disorder of vestibular function |
| **H81.4** | Vertigo of central origin |
| **F45.8** | Other somatoform disorders |
| **R42** | Dizziness and giddiness |

In [ICD-11](38_pppg_mkb.html), PPPD has its own code — **AB32.0** — and as healthcare systems transition to the new classification, the diagnosis will become more straightforward.

### Should You Stay on Sick Leave for a Long Time?

No. A week or two during a severe flare-up — that's reasonable. A month — that's already risky. Extended home rest with PPPD actually makes symptoms worse, because the brain interprets isolation as confirmation that there's danger. Get out of the house. Work. Live.

## Driving

The question everyone's afraid to ask.

With PPPD, you **will not lose consciousness behind the wheel**. The mechanism of PPPD is sensory noise, not loss of postural tone or an epileptic seizure. Fainting from PPPD is physiologically impossible.

But there are nuances:

- **Brain fog** can slow your reaction time. If you feel like you're "wrapped in cotton wool" — skip the busy highways in the dark.
- **Flickering headlights and road markings** — trigger [visual dependence](08_visual_dependence.html). Driving at night in the rain is maximum visual overload.
- **Stop-and-go traffic** — flashing brake lights ahead can increase discomfort.

Recommendation: at peak symptoms, temporarily hold off on long drives and night driving. Daytime city driving is usually fine. As you recover, the restrictions lift.

## Military Service

If you're of draft age and have PPPD — here's what you need to know.

In many countries, military fitness assessments evaluate vestibular function. If you have documented vestibular dysfunction, this can be grounds for exemption or deferral. Specifically:

- **Vestibular disorders** — if there is documented vestibular dysfunction, you may qualify for a limited fitness category (not called up during peacetime) or a medical deferral.
- **Neurotic/anxiety disorders** — if PPPD is accompanied by an anxiety disorder or panic attacks, this can also support your case.

### What You'll Need

1. Inpatient evaluation at a neurology department — with a discharge summary and diagnosis
2. MRI results, vestibular testing (VNG/ENG), audiometry
3. A report from a neuro-otologist or neurologist specifying PPPD (or the closest ICD-10 equivalent)
4. Documented functional impairment: DHI > 40, HADS-A > 11

Military medical boards may not be familiar with the diagnosis yet — be prepared to explain. Print out the Bárány Society diagnostic criteria, show the AB32.0 code in ICD-11.

## PPPD Is Temporary

All these questions — work, sick leave, driving, military service — are about "living with PPPD." But PPPD is not a life sentence. It's a functional glitch that responds to treatment within 2–6 months.
