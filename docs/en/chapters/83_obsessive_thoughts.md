# Obsessive Thoughts and OCD in PPPD: When Your Head Won't Let Go

"What if it's a tumor?"
"What if I collapse right here on the subway?"
"What if I pass out in the middle of a meeting?"

Sound familiar? Welcome to the club. I spent months living with this radio in my head that I couldn't switch off.

## When Your Brain Skips Like a Scratched Record

With PPPD, your nervous system is like a computer with a frozen process. One thread is constantly scanning your body for threats. It detects a slight swaying — launches panic. Panic amplifies the swaying. The swaying confirms the "threat."

The loop is closed. And it keeps spinning, spinning, spinning — like an infinite while(true) loop with no exit condition.

These obsessive thoughts aren't a sign of insanity. They're a bug in the monitoring system. Your brain has cranked the "search for danger" filter to maximum sensitivity — and now it finds danger in every signal your body sends.

Your heart beats a little harder — "heart attack." Your head gets slightly foggy — "stroke." Your stomach gurgles — "cancer." A muscle twitches — "multiple sclerosis."

I remember sitting up at night googling "cerebellar tumor symptoms" for the third time that week. Every time I found a new symptom that "matched." Every time I convinced myself the doctors had missed it. In the morning I'd go to work with bloodshot eyes, feeling like I was living my last days.

## Is This OCD or Just Anxiety?

True obsessive-compulsive disorder is when someone washes their hands until they bleed or checks the lock twenty times. Their obsessive thoughts aren't tied to a real physical sensation. They fear abstract "contamination" or an "unlocked door."

With PPPD, the mechanism is different. You're not afraid of something abstract — you're afraid of a specific sensation you're feeling right now. The dizziness is real. The unsteadiness is real. Your brain is simply misinterpreting the cause.

But the loop is similar: obsessive thought → anxiety → checking (Google, doctor, blood pressure measurement) → temporary relief → new thought. This isn't OCD in the classic sense. It's anxious rumination — your brain is chewing the same gum over and over because it hasn't gotten an answer that satisfies it.

As the saying goes, you can't bless every sneeze — but your brain sure tries.

## Why "Just Don't Think About It" Doesn't Work

Try not thinking about a white bear. Did it work? Exactly.

Thought suppression is the worst strategy. Research shows: the harder you push a thought away, the more often it comes back. This is called the "rebound effect."

The brain works like this: if you're actively fighting a thought, it flags the thought as important. "If the boss is this stressed about it — the threat must be real. Better send more reminders." And it does. Again and again. Every day. Like an annoying push notification you swipe away, but it keeps coming back.

## What Actually Helps: The Metacognitive Approach

There's a technique that was a game-changer for me. It's called "defusion" — unhooking from the thought. The idea is simple: you don't argue with the thought and you don't suppress it. You simply change your relationship with it.

The thought "I have a tumor" comes — you say to yourself: "Oh, hello. That's my anxious brain generating scenarios again. Fine, let it generate. I'm busy." You don't analyze. You don't Google. You don't check.

You just notice it — and get back to what you were doing. Like spam in your inbox: saw it → didn't open it → trash.

The first few days it feels impossible. The thought doesn't just knock — it kicks the door down. The thought screams, demands attention. But every time you don't feed it with a reaction, it weakens. Like a spam campaign: if you don't open the emails — over time, they come less often.

The second technique is "postponed worry." Set aside 15 minutes a day — "worry time." A thought pops up at 10 AM? "Thanks, I'll note it down and worry about it at 7 PM." Sounds absurd, but it works. You're not suppressing the thought — you're postponing the reaction. And by 7 PM, half of those "terrifying" thoughts have already gone stale.

The third tool — stop Googling symptoms. Completely. Totally. This isn't information-seeking — it's a compulsion. Every trip to Google reinforces the cycle. You're not looking for knowledge — you're looking for reassurance. And it never comes, because an anxious brain will always find a new reason to panic.

Delete your bookmarks to medical forums. Seriously. Right now. And that WebMD app too.

## What I Didn't Know Back Then

When it was hitting me hardest, I thought I was the only one. That normal people don't have thoughts like this. That if I'm constantly thinking about diseases — it means I'm really sick or going crazy.

Then I learned that obsessive health-related thoughts are one of the most common symptoms of anxiety disorder. That millions of people have been through this. That it has a name, a mechanism, and most importantly — a way out.

## The Key Insight

Obsessive thoughts in PPPD don't mean you're losing your mind. It means your nervous system is stuck in threat-scanning mode. These thoughts are like notifications from an antivirus that's flagging "viruses" in system files. The problem isn't the files — the problem is the scanner settings.

You don't have to believe every thought your brain generates. A thought is just an electrical impulse, not a verdict. I spent months fighting them before I realized: you don't need to fight. You need to stop taking them at face value.
