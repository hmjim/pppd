# CBT for Dizziness: How Working with Thoughts Eliminates Unsteadiness

*The idea that "talking to a therapist" can eliminate physical unsteadiness sounds absurd to you. You're not making it up — you REALLY are swaying. But here's the paradox: it's precisely your thoughts about the swaying that make it unbearable. Let's break down how this works.*

---

## "What do thoughts have to do with it if my legs are giving out?"

I thought the same thing. When I was first told "you need cognitive behavioral therapy" — I nearly burst out laughing. Seriously? I need to FIX MY HEAD, not TALK about feelings. The floor is sliding out from under my feet, and you're suggesting I keep a thought diary?

And then I understood one thing that changed everything.

Unsteadiness in PPPD isn't just "a brain glitch." It's a **vicious cycle** where thoughts and body amplify each other:

```
Micro-sway → "I'm about to fall!" → adrenaline → neck muscles lock up →
sensor desync → stronger swaying → "See! I told you!" →
more adrenaline → even stronger...
```

CBT strikes at the only point where you can break into this cycle — the **thought**. Not the ear, not the muscles — the interpretation. Because it's the thought "I'm about to fall" that turns a harmless wobble into a hurricane.

---

## Three Targets: What Exactly CBT Fixes in PPPD

### 1. Catastrophizing — you see death where there is none

You sway slightly on an escalator. A healthy person wouldn't even notice — their brain filters it out as background noise. But your brain is in hypervigilance mode. It interprets the micro-sway as the beginning of a catastrophe:

- *"I wobbled — I'm about to collapse"*
- *"My head is spinning — it's a stroke"*
- *"My vision went dark — I'm going to pass out"*

None of these thoughts match reality. You won't fall — your vestibular system is intact. There won't be a stroke — your MRI is clean. You won't pass out — during panic, blood pressure RISES, it doesn't drop. But the brain believes these thoughts as facts — and triggers the adrenaline cycle.

CBT teaches you to catch that thought by the tail and ask it one question: **"Is this a fact or a fear?"**

### 2. Avoidance — you're teaching your brain that the world is dangerous

You felt sick at the supermarket. You ran home. The brain noted: "Supermarket = danger. Next time, trigger panic earlier so the boss runs away faster."

A week later, you're steering clear of that supermarket. A month later — you're afraid of any supermarket. Three months later — you won't go farther than your own yard. Every act of avoidance **cements** the fear.

CBT works the opposite way: **exposure**. You go to the scary place. You sway. You stand your ground. You don't run. After 5 minutes, the adrenaline burns out. The brain checks the box: "Went to the supermarket. Didn't die. Maybe it's not that dangerous." Repeat 10 times — and the neural pathway of fear gets overgrown with weeds.

More details: [The Adrenaline Loop — the 5-Minute Rule](09_adrenaline_loop.html).

### 3. Crutches — you're not letting your brain discover it can cope

You only walk along walls. You grip the shopping cart in the store. You carry phenazepam "just in case." You don't leave the house without your spouse. This is **safety behavior** — crutches that prevent your brain from realizing it's capable of maintaining balance on its own.

As long as you're gripping the cart — your brain thinks: "Without the cart, we'll fall. The cart is saving our life." Take away the cart — and after 10 minutes of panic, your brain will discover that your legs work just fine without it. That IS the treatment.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

You don't need a psychotherapist (although it's faster with one). CBT for PPPD is a set of specific techniques you do yourself:

### Step 1: Catch the thought
When unsteadiness hits — stop and ask: "What thought just flashed through my mind?" Usually it's one of three: "I'll fall," "I feel awful," or "This will never end." Write it down.

### Step 2: Check the facts
- "I'll fall" → Have you ever actually fallen from PPPD? No? Then it's not a fact, it's a fear.
- "I feel awful" → You feel uncomfortable. Uncomfortable ≠ awful. Uncomfortable ≠ dangerous.
- "This will never end" → People recover in 3–6 months. You know this. The thought is lying.

### Step 3: Exposure
Make a list of places you avoid. Rank them from "a little scary" to "terrifyingly scary." Start with the first one. Go there. Stand. Don't run. Let the adrenaline burn out. Repeat until it becomes boring. Move on to the next one.

### Step 4: Remove the crutches
One crutch per week. First week — stop holding onto walls. Second — leave your phone in your pocket (don't call your mom at the first wobble). Third — go alone, without a companion.

## Evidence Base

CBT for functional disorders is not "alternative medicine." It's the method with the strongest evidence base:

- **Van Dessel meta-analysis (2014)**, Cochrane Library: CBT is the most effective method for functional somatic disorders
- **Goldstein et al. (2020)**: CBT reduces the intensity of functional neurological symptoms by 40–60% within 12 weeks
- **Edelman et al. (2012)**: CBT for PPPD significantly reduces DHI (Dizziness Handicap Inventory) and HADS (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale) scores

This isn't "talking about feelings." It's systematic brain retraining. Like physical therapy — but for neural connections.
