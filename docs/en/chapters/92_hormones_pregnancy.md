# PPPD, Hormones, and Pregnancy: Why the Swaying Gets Worse Before Your Period and During Menopause

*You've held steady all week, the swaying has nearly faded, and then suddenly—three days before your period—the ground shifts beneath your feet again. Panic flares instantly: Is the treatment failing? Is it all coming back? Take a breath. Your brain isn't broken. This is predictable biochemistry, and you can plan for it.*

## Why Women Develop PPPD 2 to 3 Times More Often Than Men

The numbers don't lie: women experience **PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness)** two to three times more often than men. It has nothing to do with having "weak nerves," as doctors in clinics sometimes imply—it comes down to pure physiology.

The main player here is **estrogen**. It directly modulates **serotonin**—the neurotransmitter that keeps anxiety in check and regulates signaling in the brain's vestibular nuclei. Sex hormone receptors are even present inside the inner ear itself. When your hormone levels fluctuate, your **vestibular sensitivity** fluctuates right along with them, and your **autonomic nervous system** reacts much more sharply to any balance glitch.

## PMS and PPPD: The Autonomic Double Whammy Before Your Period

Three to seven days before menstruation starts, estrogen and progesterone levels drop sharply. When you already have **PPPD**, that drop hits your **autonomic nervous system** with a **double whammy**:

- **Your serotonin buffer drops.** Baseline anxiety climbs, **visual dependence** intensifies, and bright lights and crowds feel much more overwhelming.
- **Vestibular noise increases.** Your brain receives sharper, noisier signals from the inner ear and interprets them as a threat.
- **The adrenaline loop kicks in.** The familiar rocking scares you, you tense up, and that surge of adrenaline instantly amplifies the dizziness.

Lock in this core rule: a pre-period flare-up is not a **setback**. Your brain hasn't forgotten how to balance—your hormone levels just took a temporary dip. A couple of days after your period starts, the storm settles down on its own.

## PPPD During Pregnancy: What's Safe and What's Off-Limits

Pregnancy often brings a heavy fear that raging hormones and morning sickness will make daily life unbearable. In reality, many women feel better during pregnancy thanks to steadily elevated hormone levels—and if symptoms do flare up, safe, drug-free tools work well.

**What's safe during pregnancy:**

- **Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT).** Gentle eye and balance exercises have zero effect on the fetus, while helping your brain recalibrate.
- **Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR).** Releases physical tension and blunts adrenaline surges without medication.
- **Graded exposure.** Stepping back into everyday life gradually—without bolting or relying on **safety crutches** (safety behaviors)—keeps fear from trapping you at home.

**What you must not experiment with:**

- **SSRIs in the first trimester.** Never start, stop, or adjust antidepressant doses on your own. Any medication plan while trying to conceive—and especially during the first trimester—must be **discussed with your doctors** (both your psychiatrist and your OB-GYN).
- **DIY supplements.** Unprescribed herbs and nootropics can be dangerous. Stick to **VRT**, **PMR**, **CBT**, and evidence-based medical guidance.

## Menopause and PPPD: When Estrogen Drops

Perimenopause and **menopause** are common triggers for **PPPD** starting up or worsening. Unlike PMS, this hormonal shift plays out over months, not a couple of days.

A sustained **drop in estrogen** hits two systems at once: it raises your baseline anxiety and spikes your **vestibular sensitivity**. Hot flashes, night sweats, disrupted sleep, and a racing heart alarm your nervous system, locking your brain into anxious threat-scanning mode. Doctors often brush this off as "just aging" or "bad circulation," but useless IV drips won't fix it. What actually turns things around is proper hormone management with your doctor combined with systematic PPPD recovery work.

## What to Do: Riding Out Hormonal Swings Without Panicking

Your job is to stop declaring your progress dead every time your hormones shift. Follow a clear game plan:

- **Track your cycle in a symptom log.** Jot down your cycle day right next to your dizziness score. After two or three months, you'll see a repeating pattern—and the fear of "out-of-nowhere" swaying will disappear.
- **Don't panic over flare-ups on predictable days.** Remind yourself: "This isn't the condition coming back—it's a scheduled hormonal dip."
- **Don't reach for your safety crutches.** Dial down the pace if you're having a rough day, but don't take to your bed—keep up short walks and your baseline **VRT**.
- **Check your baseline bloodwork.** Heavy periods, pregnancy, and menopause frequently trigger low ferritin and thyroid imbalances, both of which sabotage your balance independently of PPPD.
