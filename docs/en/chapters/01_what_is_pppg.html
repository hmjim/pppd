What is PPPD: symptoms, causes and why you are here

Spoiler: nothing bad happens to you. Your hardware is fine. The software fails.

Short answer

PPPD is when you are shaking, staggering, spinning, the picture is floating, your legs are weak, your head is heavy, your floor is unstable - but all tests are normal. MRI is clear. Blood is fine. Ears are working. The eyes see.

Doctors shrug their shoulders. But this doesn’t make it any easier for you - because it really pumps you up.

PPPD is Persistent Postural-Perceptual Vertigo. In the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) it is coded as AA36. In English - PPPD (Persistent Postural-Perceptual Dizziness). This is not an invention of psychologists. This is an official medical diagnosis.

But here's what's important to understand right now: it's a functional disorder. Not organic. Your brain or ear structure is not broken. The function of their work has changed. The program crashed, but the hardware was intact.

PPPD symptoms: complete list

People with PPPD describe their experiences in dozens of different ways. If you recognize yourself in at least 3-4 points, you are in the right place.

Main symptoms:

  • Feeling of unsteadiness and instability when walking
  • “Cotton” or “cotton-cotton” legs
  • Feeling that the floor is shaking and disappearing from under your feet
  • Fog in the head, “cotton wool” in the head, heavy head
  • Feeling of swaying even when sitting or lying down
  • “Floating” picture before your eyes
  • Increased symptoms in stores, shopping centers, metro
  • Discomfort from colorful floors, striped patterns, scrolling phone

Associated symptoms:

  • Anxiety and panic attacks
  • Derealization - the world as “through glass” or “in a dream”
  • Depersonalization - a feeling of unreality of one’s own body
  • Insomnia and shallow sleep
  • Morning unsteadiness (worst in the morning)
  • Tension and pain in the neck, shoulders, jaw
  • Tinnitus (tinnitus) with high anxiety
  • Photosensitivity and intolerance to bright lighting
  • Obsessive “scanning” of the body for symptoms

What is NOT a symptom of PPPD:

  • Loss of consciousness (fainting)
  • Sharp rotational dizziness for 5–60 seconds (more like BPPV)
  • Hearing loss in one ear
  • Double vision
  • Speech or swallowing problems

If you have anything from the last list, see a doctor first. PPPD is a diagnosis of exclusion.

A little history (without boring)

For a long time, people like us did not exist in medicine at all. You went to the doctor, said “I’m shaking,” and he answered: “Well, it’s nervous” - and that’s all. Invisible.

Since the 1980s, it has been called "phobic postural vertigo." Then - “space discomfort” (sounds like a diagnosis for an astronaut). And only in 2014 the World Organization of Neurosciences finally collected all these scraps into one normal definition. In 2017 the Barany Society published 5 clear diagnostic criteria.

Since then, PPPD has been a legitimate functional vestibular disorder. Not "nervous". Not “it seems to you.” A specific diagnosis with a specific code.

Causes of PPPD: how a failure occurs (IT language)

I’ll explain it the way I explain it to everyone.

Your body is hardware (iron). The brain, ears, eyes, muscles, nerves are physical components. And the psyche and neural connections are software (software).

PPPD is not a failure of parts. This is a software glitch.

Imagine: on your computer, the “antivirus” (alarm) is running at 100% processor power. He fanatically scans every file (body sensation) for a deadly threat. The processor is overheating. The fan is howling. The system starts to freeze.

And instead of closing the antivirus, you run to the service center to change the hard drive. Then you change the RAM. Then the motherboard. And nothing helps, because the problem is not in the hardware.

The problem is in the software, which crashed due to overvoltage. And we have to reinstall it.

Three balance sensors

The brain has three systems that are responsible for balance:

  1. Eyes (vision) - you see where is up, where is down, whether the world is moving around you.
  2. Vestibular apparatus (gyroscope in the inner ear) - senses turns and tilts of the head.
  3. Proprioception (sense of the body in space) - muscles and joints tell the brain where the arms, legs, and head are.

Normally, the brain automatically combines data from all three. You don't even think about it. Just like you don’t think about breathing or heartbeat.

But when you are in distress for a long time - unsolvable problems, exhaustion, chronic stress - the brain goes into “DEADLY DANGER” mode. Cortisol and adrenaline rush into the blood. The muscles spasm. And this is where the fun begins.

Metaphor of a pinched neck

This is the key point. Remember it.

Receptors in the neck muscles—proprioceptors—tell the brain where the head is. When the neck is pinched from chronic stress, these receptors are squeezed along with it. Like a wire caught in a doorframe.

The signal from them goes to the brain with a microdelay.

The eyes see: you are coming. The gyroscope in the ear senses that you are moving. And the pinched neck says: “We’re still there!”

This desynchronization—in milliseconds—is interpreted by the brain as dizziness and unsteadiness. You feel “cotton wool” in your head. The illusion of falling. Feeling like the floor is floating.

And you run to the MRI in panic, and the MRI shows: everything is clear. Because the problem is not the structure. Signal problem.

Why massage doesn't help for long

Many people go to a massage therapist. He stretches his stiff neck. It becomes easier for a day or two. And then everything comes back.

Because you are massaging a spasming muscle, and the command to tense it continues to come from your frightened brain. It's like mopping up a puddle on the floor without turning off the tap. Pointless.

Massage is good. But it only works in conjunction with the rest. We'll get there.

PPPD is a reversible condition

Let me say it straight so you stop being afraid.

In more than two years of running the group, no one died from this. No one had a blood vessel burst. No stroke. No heart attack. No epilepsy. One person fell - in the gym. He got up, got up and went on to do his business. The only recorded case.

PPPD is a completely reversible condition. There are people who have been at this for 3 months. There are some for six months. There is someone 5 years old. There are some 20. Our longest case is 30 years. You can get stuck in this. But you can also get out. And it's not a matter of luck - it's a matter of work.

Derealization: fuse

If, in addition to instability, your world has also become “unreal” - like through glass, like in a dream, like in a movie - this is derealization. Or depersonalization (when you yourself seem “unreal”).

Don't be scared. This is a fuse.

When anxiety gets high, the brain desensitizes itself to protect you. Like a fuse in an electrical panel, it turns off the system so that the wiring does not burn out. It's not crazy. This is a defensive reaction to extreme stress.

When the level of anxiety decreases, derealization will go away on its own. We'll get there.

Main conclusion

You are not organically ill. Your hardware is intact. This is a software glitch caused by chronic stress and anxiety.

PPPD is not a death sentence. It's not "for life." This is a specific problem with a specific solution. And we will start solving it right now.

PPPD subtypes: which one do you have?

PPPD is not one monolithic diagnosis. It manifests itself differently in different people. There are three main subtypes:

1. Posturally induced: Unsteadiness increases in a vertical position - standing and when walking. Sitting and lying down are much easier. This is the most common subtype. The brain “does not trust” proprioception and tightens the muscles of the legs and back, trying to maintain balance manually.

2. Visually-induced: The main trigger is a complex visual environment: supermarkets, colorful tiles, scrolling your phone, flashing outside a bus window. The visual system is overloaded, and the vestibular system is “muffled” - the brain cannot filter out visual noise.

3. Mixed: A combination of both subtypes. It’s hard to stand, and the visual environment is unsettling. This is the most common option - about 60% of people with PPPD.

Determine your subtype - this will help you prioritize your practice. If you are visually induced, focus on Chapter 8 (Visual Addiction) and VOR gymnastics. If posturally induced, prioritize Chapter 6 (Vestibular Calibration) and proprioceptive exercises.

Important: If you have hearing loss, noise in one ear, sudden attacks of true rotation or loss of consciousness - this is not PPPD, and you need to see an otoneurologist. PPPD is a diagnosis of exclusion: it is made when all “serious” causes have already been rejected.

Frequently asked questions

“I was diagnosed with protrusion/osteochondrosis/VAD (autonomic dysfunction). Maybe it’s from them?” No. Osteochondrosis occurs in 90% of people over 30 years of age. Protrusion - in 70%. These are radiographic findings that are not clinically significant in most cases. VAD (autonomic dysfunction) is an outdated Soviet diagnosis that is not found in either ICD-10 or ICD-11. This is the same neurosis, only with a different name.

“Or maybe I have a cerebrovascular accident?” If you are under 60 years old, you do not have hypertension, diabetes mellitus and atherosclerosis, the likelihood of vascular vertigo tends to zero. An ultrasound scan of the neck vessels will show the norm (or “S-shaped bend of the vertebral artery” - a variant of the norm, which is present in 40% of the population).

“I was prescribed a bunch of nootropics. Will they help?" Mexidol, Actovegin, Cerebrolysin, Cavinton, betahistine - none of these drugs has proven effectiveness in PPPD. This does not mean that you cannot take them - if your doctor prescribes them, discuss them with him. But don't expect healing from them. The only evidence-based drugs for PPPD are SSRIs (Chapter 13), and then as an addition to therapy, not a replacement.
