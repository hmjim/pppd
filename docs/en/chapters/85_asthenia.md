# Asthenia and Chronic Fatigue in Neurosis: Battery Full, Charger Broken

You slept ten hours. Woke up feeling like you'd been unloading freight cars all night. Had coffee — didn't help. Lay down — didn't help. Took a week off — first two days seemed a little better, then on the third you were a wrung-out lemon again.

Sound familiar? That's asthenia. And rest won't cure it.

## Why You're Exhausted Even Though You Did Nothing

Imagine your phone has thirty apps running in the background. The screen is off, you're not touching it — but the battery dies in two hours. You think: "That's weird, I'm not doing anything." But the phone is. It's updating, syncing, scanning in the background.

Your brain during anxiety is that phone. On the outside, you're lying on the couch. On the inside, your nervous system is continuously monitoring your body, analyzing every signal, bracing for "danger" that doesn't exist. This background process devours energy like a mining farm.

You're not lazy. You're drained from the inside. And no amount of rest fixes it — because you're resting your body, while it's your brain that's exhausted.

## What's Happening with Your Hormones

Under chronic stress, the HPA axis (hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal) first works at full throttle. Cortisol is high, adrenaline is high, you're in "live wire under voltage" mode.

You can't sleep, you don't want to eat, but there's some energy — nervous, jittery, but it's there.

But the body can't run in emergency mode forever.

After a few months, the system "burns out." The morning cortisol spike — which normally gets you out of bed feeling energized — flattens. Instead of a morning boost, you get an even swamp of fatigue from dawn to midnight. Doctors call this "flattening of the cortisol curve."

This isn't adrenal insufficiency or Addison's disease. It's a functional glitch from the system running at redline for too long.

Like an engine that's been revved in the red zone on the tachometer — it's not broken, but it's overheated and now barely crawls in first gear.

Meanwhile, dopamine drops — the neurotransmitter of motivation and pleasure. Hobbies, hanging out with friends, interesting projects at work used to make you happy. Now even your favorite things provoke a reaction of "I don't care, leave me alone."

This isn't classic depression — it's exhaustion of the reward system. The brain spent all its resources on anxiety and has nothing left for joy.

## Myths That Make Things Worse

"You just need more rest." If the problem were lack of rest, a week of sleep would solve it. But you're not physically tired — you're neurologically depleted. Lying on the couch doesn't shut down the background processes in your brain. It often makes things worse: lying there, you think more, worry more, scroll your phone, spiral deeper.

"Take some vitamins, get your iron checked." Worth checking — iron deficiency and low vitamin D genuinely cause fatigue. But if your labs are normal and you're still wiped out — vitamins won't help. They're like gas for a car with an overheated engine. The tank is full, but it still can't go.

"It's your age." You could be 25 or 35 — asthenia from anxiety doesn't check your ID. I've seen twenty-year-olds who could barely crawl around their apartment, and fifty-year-olds running half-marathons. The difference isn't years — it's the state of the nervous system.

"Just pull yourself together." With what? Which hands? The ones already shaking from exhaustion? That's like telling someone with a broken leg: "Just walk." Willpower is a resource, and yours is at zero. You can't spend what you don't have.

## How to Tell Asthenia from Normal Tiredness

Simple test. Normal tiredness: you're tired → you rest → you recover. Logical, linear, predictable.

Asthenia from anxiety: you're tired → you rest → you're even more tired. Or: you're tired → you sleep → you wake up wrecked → the day passes — by evening you feel slightly better → you go to bed — morning hits and you're a zombie again.

If rest doesn't restore you — it's not tiredness. It's nervous system exhaustion.

## What Actually Helps

The paradox: with nervous exhaustion, what helps isn't rest — it's the right kind of activity.

Light physical activity — walking, swimming, yoga — reboots the autonomic nervous system. It forces your body to shift from "freeze" mode to "move" mode. I know, when you don't have the energy to get off the couch, the advice to "go for a walk" sounds like mockery.

But it works. Start with five minutes. Not a marathon — five minutes of walking around the block. Tomorrow, six. The day after, seven. In a month you'll be walking for an hour and amazed that it's possible.

I started by walking to the nearest shop — 200 meters. I'd come back and lie down. The next day I went a little farther. Two months later I was walking 10 kilometers a day. Not because I got "stronger" — but because my nervous system started to shift gears.

Second — address the anxiety that triggers this whole cascade. As long as your brain is in constant threat-scanning mode, no amount of rest will help. It's like trying to fill a bathtub with the drain open — no matter how much you pour in, the level won't rise.

## The Key Insight

Your fatigue isn't laziness, weak character, or something you can fix by "pulling yourself together." It's real nervous exhaustion from your brain running in emergency mode for months. The battery isn't dead — the charger is broken. And it's the charger that needs fixing, not the battery.
