PPPD While Lying Down: Why the Bed Rocks Like You're on a Boat

You lie down to sleep, close your eyes — and the bed starts to "drift." Like a boat on the open sea. Except you're in your own apartment, on your own mattress, and there's no sea anywhere in sight. Sound familiar? Let's break it down.

Why the Rocking Is Worse in Bed Than on Your Feet

It sounds paradoxical: during the day you walk around, get stuff done, it's manageable. But in the evening you lie down — and off it goes. The bed sways, the floor drops out, a sensation of falling. You'd think lying down should be easier, but no.

Here's why. When you're standing or walking, your brain receives a flood of information:

  • Your eyes see walls, the floor, the horizon — visual anchoring.
  • Your feet press against the floor, leg muscles are working — proprioceptive anchoring.
  • The vestibular system provides its share of data.

Three streams. The brain cross-references them and manages to keep the picture more or less stable.

Now you lie down in bed. Turn off the light. Close your eyes.

Boom. Two out of three streams — gone. Eyes closed — visual anchoring is out. Body relaxed on a soft mattress — proprioception is minimal. All that's left are the vestibular nuclei and the brain's own "noise."

And with PPPD, the vestibular centers are running in hypersensitivity mode. It's like cranking the volume on your speakers to maximum in an empty room. Silence? Nope — you hear hissing, crackling, humming. That's background electrical noise. At normal volume you never notice it, but at max — it's deafening.

Same thing with the brain. Without external anchors, it starts amplifying its own background signal: your pulse, your breathing, micro-movements of your body. And it interprets all of this as powerful rocking. You're "riding the waves" even though you're lying perfectly still.

Anxious Scanning Before Sleep

But that's not all. There's a second layer.

You lie down — and what's the first thing you do? You listen in. "So, is it rocking? Is it about to start? Come on, come on..."

This is the CAS trap in its purest form. You direct your attention inward and wait for the symptom. The brain gets the command: "The boss is looking for danger → it must exist → activate alarm."

What happens next:

  • The limbic system fires up
  • A dose of adrenaline is released
  • Neck muscles reflexively tighten
  • The rocking sensation intensifies dramatically

You're triggering the very thing you fear. Like the old joke: "Doctor, when I do this — it hurts." "Well, don't do that."

What to Do When the Rocking Hits While Lying Down

Don't Close Your Eyes in a Panic

Turn on a soft nightlight. Find a stationary point on the wall or ceiling with your eyes. Fix your gaze on it for 1–2 minutes. You're giving your brain a visual anchor — and it calms down. Three streams are better than one.

Tactile Grounding

Place your palms on the wall next to the bed. Or on a wooden headboard. Feel the texture, the temperature, the stillness of the surface. You can physically feel it: the wall is solid. The bed is solid. There's no rocking. Only neural noise.

Paradoxical Intention

Instead of "please make it stop rocking" — say: "Okay, brain. Rock away. Show me what you've got. I'm lying on a solid bed, I physically cannot fall off. Let it rock."

The moment you stop resisting — the anxiety loop breaks. You remove fear from the equation. And without fear, the adrenaline loop stalls.

This is Viktor Frankl's technique — paradoxical intention. Give the symptom permission to exist. And it loses its power over you.

Breathing

Slow diaphragmatic breathing. Inhale through your nose for 4 counts. Hold for 2. Exhale through your mouth for 6–8 counts. The long exhale activates the parasympathetic system and physically reduces adrenaline release.

You don't need to do this for 30 minutes. 5–7 minutes is enough to reset your nervous system.

About Mornings

By the way, morning unsteadiness is the same story. After a night of sleep, while the brain was "offline," vestibular calibration resets. The first 30–60 minutes after getting up are the foggiest. That's normal. Don't panic. It usually eases up by midday.

A full breakdown of the connection between sleep and PPPD is in the chapter "Sleep and PPPD."

The Rocking Will Go Away

It will go away. Not instantly — but it will. When your baseline anxiety drops, the brain will stop amplifying its own noise, and the evening rocking in bed will be one of the first things to disappear.

For me, it went away around the third month of doing the work. One evening I just lay down — and silence. No rocking, no waves. Just lying there. And falling asleep like a normal person.
