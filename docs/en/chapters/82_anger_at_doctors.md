# Anger at Doctors: How to Stop Hating the Ones Who Didn't Listen

## "You're Fine, Go Home"

You're sitting in a neurologist's office. The third one in six months. You've brought a folder of test results two fingers thick. Brain MRI, cervical MRI, Doppler ultrasound, audiogram, blood work for everything under the sun. The doctor flips through it for thirty seconds, closes the folder, and says: "Everything's normal. Take some Mexidol and go for more walks. Next!"

And you walk into the hallway with that folder in your hands and fury in your throat. Because you are NOT fine. You're dizzy every single day. You can't work, sleep, or live normally. But the person in the white coat, the one you entrusted your life to, just told you: you're making it up.

I went through twelve doctors in two years. Twelve. Each one had their own diagnosis: VSD, cervical osteochondrosis, "burnout," "nerves," "have you tried seeing a psychiatrist?" One prescribed Actovegin for three months. Another — Betaserc for six months. A third — "take some vitamins and don't stress." Not a single one said the word PPPD.

I remember one especially well. He looked at my scans, raised an eyebrow, and said: "Young man, you have cervical osteochondrosis. Everyone over twenty-five has it. Exercise more, it'll go away." I was exercising. It didn't go away. But he never found that out — because patient number thirty-one was more interesting than patient number thirty.

## Why It Cuts So Deep

With doctors, there's a special kind of trust at play. When you go to a doctor, you make yourself vulnerable. You're essentially saying: "I can't handle this, help me." It's like handing over root access to your system. And when the person you gave root privileges to says "the problem doesn't exist" — that's not just an error. It's a betrayal at the architectural level of trust.

Research by psychologist Judith Herman shows that betrayal by an authority figure is processed by the brain as institutional trauma. It's not just hurt feelings — it's a rewiring of your entire map of "who in this world can be trusted."

And the anger you feel is absolutely justified. You weren't heard. You were dismissed. You were treated for something you don't have, and not treated for what you do. You have every right to be furious.

And on top of that — you lost time. Months. Years. While they fed you Mexidol and "vitamins," the illness was digging in. Anxiety-driven neural pathways were growing deeper. And that loss of time — that's what infuriates you most. Because time can't be returned.

## A System Bug, Not Malice

But here's what you need to understand — and it's going to hurt. Those doctors, most likely, weren't evil. They were untrained.

PPPD as a diagnosis was only ratified in the International Classification in 2017. Most medical schools in the post-Soviet region still don't teach it. A neurologist who trained in 2005 simply doesn't know what functional dizziness is. In their worldview, dizziness is either BPPV, or Ménière's, or a tumor. If none of those were found — the patient is healthy. Not because the doctor is an idiot. Because their database has no entry for your condition.

VSD — vegetovascular dystonia — doesn't even exist as a diagnosis in the international classification. But in post-Soviet medicine, it's a junk drawer where they toss everything they can't explain. Convenient? For the doctor — yes. For you — a catastrophe.

Mexidol, Actovegin, Cerebrolysin — drugs with unproven efficacy, prescribed out of inertia. "Well, you have to prescribe something." This isn't treatment. It's a ritual. Like rebooting a computer when you don't know what's wrong — maybe it'll help.

And Betaserc? Betahistine works for Ménière's disease. For PPPD — there's no evidence base. But they prescribe it to everyone because "well, it's for dizziness." It's like running antivirus software during a hardware failure: software won't fix a bent memory stick.

The result: you take pills for months, you don't get better, and you start thinking you're a lost cause. When in reality — you were simply being treated for the wrong thing.

## The Poison You Drink Yourself

Here's the heartbreaking paradox: your anger at doctors is justified and destructive at the same time.

Every time you replay that neurologist appointment in your head — the one who brushed you off — you trigger a stress response. Cortisol. Adrenaline. Muscle tension. The vestibular system reacts. Symptoms intensify. You're literally making yourself worse by mentally arguing with a doctor who forgot your name long ago.

As the saying goes: "Holding onto anger is like drinking poison and expecting the other person to die." Blunt wisdom, but accurate. Holding anger at doctors is like running a malicious process on your own system. It doesn't harm the server you're angry at. It devours only your resources.

Forgiveness here isn't about them. It's entirely and completely about you. You forgive not because they were right. You forgive to free up RAM for recovery.

I couldn't accept that for a long time. It felt like forgiving meant justifying. It doesn't. Forgiving means stopping the process "mental argument with Dr. Ivanov" from running three hundred times a week. It's not about his being right. It's about your peace.

## How to Rebuild Your Relationship with Medicine

Letting go of the anger doesn't mean blindly trusting again. It means shifting from the "doctor-as-god" model to the "doctor-as-contractor" model.

You are the client. You have the spec (your symptoms). The doctor is the contractor who has expertise but not the final word. You have the right to ask questions, request evidence, and move on to another specialist. Not with resentment — with pragmatism.

Specific questions worth asking a new doctor: "Are you familiar with the diagnosis of PPPD?" "What's your experience with functional disorders?" "What's the evidence base for the medication you're prescribing?" If a doctor gets irritated by these questions — they're not your doctor. A good specialist welcomes an informed patient.

Look for doctors who know what functional disorders are. Otoneurologists, vestibular specialists, neurologists who've done training on PPPD. They exist. There aren't many, but they exist. And one right doctor is worth twenty wrong ones.

## Homework: A Letter to Your Worst Doctor

Write a letter. To that very doctor who didn't hear you. The one after whom you walked out with a lump in your throat and the feeling you were losing your mind.

Write everything. "You told me I was healthy, and then I couldn't leave the house for six months." "You spent four minutes on me and wrecked half a year of my life." "I hate your indifferent voice." Don't filter. Don't be polite. This letter is for you, not for them.

Then add a second paragraph. "I understand that you most likely didn't know about my diagnosis. That you weren't taught this. That you had fifteen minutes per appointment and thirty patients behind the door. That's not an excuse — but it's an explanation."

And a final paragraph: "I'm taking my energy back. I'm not going to mentally argue with you at three in the morning anymore. I need that energy for getting better."

Read it out loud. And then — burn it. Literally. A match, an ashtray, fire. This isn't mysticism. It's a closure ritual. The brain closes open loops better when there's a physical action. Like hitting Ctrl+S — until you save, the system considers the process still running.

If you can't burn it — tear it into tiny pieces. Or delete the file from your computer. The physical destruction of the text gives the brain a signal: "This process is complete. Resources freed."

Don't send the letter. This isn't about them. It's about you finally killing that hung process and freeing up resources for living.

And one more thing. If the anger doesn't fully leave after the letter — that's fine. It doesn't have to leave all at once. Write another one in a month. And another in two. Each time there'll be fewer letters and more free RAM. The process will complete when it completes. Your job isn't to suppress the anger but to give it an outlet that doesn't poison you from the inside.
